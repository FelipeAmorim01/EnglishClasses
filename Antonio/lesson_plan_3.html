Stage 02 of 06
Fill in the blanks
You see a sentence with one word half-written: the first letters, then a box for each missing letter. You have about 20 seconds. The words around the gap tell you which word it is.
How to find the word
Read the whole sentence
The clue is often after the gap, not before it. Read to the full stop first.
red___ the risk of heart disease → reduce
Words come in pairs
English has fixed partners (collocations). Learn the pair, not just the word.
make a decision · pay attention · heavy traffic
Count the boxes
Each box is one letter. Two words can start the same way, but only one has the right length.
evi + 5 boxes → evidence, not evident
Check the grammar last
Right word, wrong form still loses the point. Plural? Past tense? Noun or verb?
one of the main causes, not cause

Stage 03 of 06
Interactive reading
One passage, several questions about it, about 7–8 minutes in all. On the test the questions come one screen at a time and the exact parts can vary, but the skills are the same: read fast, then find the exact words.
Three steps
Do it in this order
  1. Skim first, about 30 seconds. Read the first line of each paragraph and ask: what is this paragraph for?
  2. Find the keyword, then the exact words. Take a word from the question, find it in the passage, and highlight only the words that answer, not the whole sentence.
  3. Main idea and title last. By then you've read everything. The right answer covers the whole passage, not one paragraph.

Stage 04 of 06
Main idea & titles
The wrong answers in main-idea and title questions are wrong in the same few ways. Name the way, and you can rule them out fast.
Three wrong answers and a right one
Too narrow
True, but it's one detail from one paragraph.
"Hospitals need staff every day."
Too broad
So general it could be the title of a whole book.
"The World of Work"
Not in the text
Sounds reasonable, but the passage never says it, or says the opposite.
"Everyone should work four days a week."
Just right
Covers every paragraph, and nothing in it goes beyond the text.
"The Four-Day Week: Promise and Limits"

FMain idea and title · Multitasking
1:00
Many people believe that doing several things at once saves time. In fact, research suggests the opposite. When we switch between tasks, such as writing an email and checking a message, the brain needs a moment to refocus each time. These small delays add up, and mistakes become more common. People who focus on one task at a time usually finish sooner and make fewer errors.
GMain idea and title · Food waste
1:00
Around the world, a large share of the food that is produced is never eaten. Some of it is lost on farms or during transport, but a lot is thrown away at home, often because people buy more than they need or misunderstand the dates on the label. Planning meals, freezing leftovers and checking what is already in the fridge are simple ways to waste less.

Read one short article in English every day. Before you read, look only at the title and guess what it's about. After reading, type the main idea in one sentence and note two word pairs you found (like "reach an agreement").

Keep going with 10 new words a day, with two other forms of each. New rule: add the word it usually goes with (decision → make a decision). Autocorrect off.

Add any words you missed today. Every day, cover the list and write five of them from memory.

Keep the daily 5-minute sprint. New rule: use at least two linking words in each one (however, as a result, for instance…). Send me two sprints this week.
