Stage 01 of 04
Warm-up: the story you always tell
Everyone has three or four stories they've told a hundred times. In your own language you know exactly where the laugh is. Today we rebuild that instinct in English.
Let's talk
  • What's the story you always end up telling? The one that comes out at every dinner.
  • Who's the best storyteller you know? Not the funniest person — the best teller. What do they actually do that holds a table?
  • When did you last make someone laugh in English? Was it something you said, or something that happened?
  • Now the other side: what makes you stop listening to someone else's story?
Stage 02 of 04
The Storyteller's Toolkit
Everything on this page is here to be used out loud in stage 3. Around sixty expressions: swaps, openers, idioms, phrasal verbs and reactions. Read them, say them, and mark the ones you've never once used in a real conversation.
Say it like a native speaker

Everything in the left column is correct English. It's just not what anyone actually says. Six patterns that close the gap between the English you were taught and the English you hear — and the third column is the one that counts. Read each of those out loud.

"He said he didn't want to do it." And he goes, "yeah — I'm not doing that." So I ask her if she's coming, and she goes, "I already told you no." And I'm like, "when?"
"I met a man at the airport who..." So there's this guy at the airport who... There's this woman who lives upstairs, and she's got this enormous dog that howls all night.
"We planned to leave at six." We were supposed to leave at six. We were supposed to land at nine. I was going to call you from the airport, but my phone was dead.
"He asked me many times." He kept asking me. She kept looking at her phone while I was talking, and the waiter kept walking past without stopping.
"It was very cold." It was absolutely freezing. By the time we got there we were absolutely freezing, completely exhausted and totally starving.
"...and other similar things." ...and stuff like that. / ...or something. So some guy comes over, asks me about the tickets or something, and we talk for a couple of minutes about work and stuff like that.
The scene in one line

Fixed expressions for opening, turning and stalling. They're formulaic on purpose — learn them whole, the way a native speaker stores them, rather than building each one from grammar.

Opening the frame
So this was back when I was living in... Right — picture this. This was, what, three years ago now? OK, so you know when you...? I don't think I've told you this one.
Signalling the turn
And this is the bit I still don't understand. And then it got weird. Now — bear in mind I had no idea. So far, so normal. And then... Anyway, that's when I realised.
Buying yourself a second
Hang on, I'm telling this badly. Let me start again. Sorry — I should have said... Where was I? Right. You need to know one thing first.
Six phrases that live in anecdotes
out of the blue
With no warning at all, from nowhere.
"And then out of the blue he asks me if I want the job."
next thing I know
Jumps you straight to the consequence and skips the boring middle.
"Next thing I know I'm in a taxi to the airport with no passport."
long story short
Signals you're cutting to the ending on purpose. Use it once — twice and it sounds like you've lost control.
"Long story short, we never got the deposit back."
of all people / of all places
Marks a coincidence as absurd. Does a lot of work for two words.
"And who's sitting there? My old boss. In Lisbon, of all places."
it went downhill from there
Everything got progressively worse after that point.
"The starter arrived cold and it went downhill from there."
you had to be there
The graceful way to close a story that didn't land. Everyone needs this one.
"...no, OK, it was funnier at the time. You had to be there."
The verbs anecdotes are made of

Twelve phrasal verbs. Almost every story you tell in English will need three or four of them, and there is no elegant way around them — the single-word alternatives all sound like a police report.

bump into / run into
To meet someone by chance. Not find, not encounter.
"I bumped into my old boss at the airport."
end up
To finish somewhere or doing something you didn't plan. Followed by -ing or a place.
"We ended up walking home at three in the morning."
turn out
The truth was different from what everyone thought. The single most useful verb in any anecdote.
"It turned out he'd never worked there at all."
show up
To arrive — often late, unexpectedly, or in the wrong state.
"Nobody showed up until half past nine."
get stuck
Unable to move or continue. Works for traffic, lifts, conversations and problems.
"We got stuck in the lift for forty minutes."
freak out
To panic or lose control emotionally. Informal, and much more natural than "I became very nervous".
"My sister completely freaked out when she saw the bill."
mess up
To do something badly or ruin it — usually your own fault.
"I messed up the booking and we lost the table."
kick off
To start — and, informally, for an argument or a scene to erupt.
"And that's when it all kicked off."
come across
To find something by chance; also, to give a certain impression.
"I came across an old photo of us in a drawer."
put up with
To tolerate something unpleasant, usually for a long time.
"I put up with that noise for two years."
back out
To withdraw from something you'd agreed to do.
"He backed out the night before the flight."
work out
To end well; also, to figure something out. Two meanings, both constant.
"In the end it all worked out." / "I never worked out why."
The other half: reacting

Listening isn't silence. In English, a listener is expected to make noise — and if you don't, the person telling the story assumes it's going badly and rushes the ending. This is the half we're going to drill hardest today.

Surprise
No way. You're kidding. He did what? Get out. Seriously?
Sympathy / wincing
Oh, that's awful. Ugh, I can imagine. That's brutal. Oh no. No, no, no.
Pushing for more
So what did you do? Wait, back up — who's this? And she just let you? Hang on, where was this? Please tell me you said something.
Closing it off
Classic. That's brilliant. I'm stealing that story. Right, my turn.
Drill: eighteen reactions, no repeats

I read you a line, you fire one back straight away — and once you've used a reaction it's gone for the rest of the drill. The first two are easy. By the tenth you'll be reaching for the ones you'd never normally touch, which is the entire point.

Hit "Next line" for a situation. React to it straight away — first thing out of your mouth.

Each reaction gets clicked the moment it's used, and a struck-through one is spent — find another.

The Story Machine
A prompt you didn't choose, a clock, and a listener who won't stay quiet. Nobody gets to tell a story in peace — so let's practise it the way it actually happens.
How it works

Draw a prompt. You get about fifteen seconds to decide which of your real stories fits it — don't invent one, dig one up. Then the clock starts and you tell it, and the only rule is that you cram in as much of stage 2 as you can. While you're talking I'll be hitting the buttons below; each one is an interruption designed to force a specific piece of English out of you. When the timer stops, we count how many of today's expressions actually made it out of your mouth.

Stage 04 of 04
Wrap-up: what actually landed
Two minutes on what changed between the first story and the last one.
What stuck
  • Close the page. How many of today's expressions can you give me from memory? Just start listing — I'll count, and I'm not helping.
  • Now open stage 3 and look at the tally. Which ones did you actually use, and which did you recognise on the page but never once say out loud? That gap is the entire lesson.
  • Of the twelve phrasal verbs, which were already in your English, and which are genuinely new? Be honest about the difference between "I've seen it" and "I'd say it."
  • Pick three expressions from today and use each one in a sentence about your own life. Now, out loud, no preparation.
  • As the listener, which reactions did you reach for? Give me two from the bank that you never touched — and a situation where you'd use them.
