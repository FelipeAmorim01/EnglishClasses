Stage 01 of 04
Warm-up: the subject you go quiet on
Money is the field where a fluent adult suddenly sounds like a beginner. You can argue a position, tell a story and run a meeting in English — and then someone asks what you paid for something and you get expensive, cheap and a good price. There are about ninety other things to say.
Let's talk
  • Best thing you've ever bought — the one that turned out to be worth every cent. And the worst.
  • Do you haggle, or do you just pay what's asked? Has that changed as you've got older?
  • Are you someone who talks about money easily, or is it a subject you go quiet on?
  • Last time you and someone else had to meet halfway on money — splitting something, agreeing a price, deciding who pays. How did it end?
  • Overpaying or being too tight — which mistake do you make more often?

The Money Toolkit
Everything here is for stage 3, where you'll need it under a bit of pressure. Read the third column of the table out loud — each sentence is built to give you the pattern two or three times in one go.

It was very expensive. it cost a fortune · it set me back (R$800) That kitchen cost us a fortune, and the oven alone set me back three thousand — which is a lot for what it is.

The value scale — five verdicts on a price
A steal
You can't believe your luck.
  • an absolute steal
  • a real bargain
  • dirt cheap
Good value
You'd pay it again tomorrow.
  • worth every penny
  • good value for money
  • it pays for itself
Fair enough
No strong feeling either way.
  • about right
  • the going rate
  • reasonable enough
  • nothing to complain about
A bit steep
You'll pay — and mention it twice.
  • a bit steep
  • pricey
  • a lot for what it is
A rip-off
You walk away.
  • daylight robbery
  • a total rip-off
Saying the figure without committing to it
Approximating
in the region of somewhere around give or take a ballpark figure anywhere between X and Y a couple of grand
Say these three out loud, twice each — once exactly, once vaguely: R$47,500 · R$1,200 · R$380. Exactly is forty-seven thousand five hundred. Vaguely is the best part of fifty grand. The second one is the harder skill and the one that actually comes up.
Idioms
cost an arm and a leg
Be painfully expensive.
The dental work cost an arm and a leg, and that was with insurance.
tighten your belt
Cut spending because you have to.
We've had to tighten our belts a bit since the rent went up.
make ends meet
Have just enough to cover what you owe.
Two jobs, and they're still barely making ends meet.
money down the drain
Spending with nothing to show for it.
Four months of a gym I never went to — money straight down the drain.
not break the bank
Not cost more than you can afford.
Somewhere decent, but nothing that's going to break the bank.
in the red / in the black
Overdrawn, losing money / back in profit.
We were in the red for most of the first year and only just went back into the black.
penny-pinching
Mean with money, in a small-minded way.
There's being careful, and there's penny-pinching — splitting a bill to the cent is the second one.
foot the bill
End up paying, usually for other people.
Guess who footed the bill for the entire weekend.
throw money at something
Spend heavily instead of fixing the real problem.
They keep throwing money at it instead of asking why it keeps happening.
Phrasal verbs
set (you) back
Cost — always with a figure after it.
A decent laptop will set you back about six thousand these days.
splash out (on)
Spend freely on something nice, deliberately.
We splashed out on business class for once, and I regret nothing.
chip in
Each contribute a share.
If everyone chips in twenty, we can cover the whole thing.
put aside / put away
Save a bit at a time, on purpose.
I put a bit aside every month without really noticing it.
run up (a bill)
Accumulate a large bill or debt.
They ran up a four-hundred-euro bar tab in one night.
pay off
Clear a debt — or turn out to be worth it.
We finally paid off the car, and the extra we spent on good tyres really paid off.
come to
Add up to, total.
That comes to three hundred and twenty altogether.
get by (on)
Manage on a limited amount.
You can get by on very little there if you don't eat out.
cut back (on)
Reduce what you spend on something.
We've cut back on eating out, not on travelling — that's the one thing we won't cut back on.
Language Tip
lend vs. borrow
Portuguese covers both directions with emprestar. English splits them into two verbs, and the only thing that separates them is which way the money is travelling. Get the direction right and the grammar follows on its own.
lend — it leaves your hand
lend someone something
lend something to someone
  • I lent him R$1,200 back in March.
  • Could you lend me twenty until Friday?
  • I lent it to a friend and never saw it again.
borrow — it comes into your hand
borrow something from someone
  • I borrowed R$1,200 from him back in March.
  • Could I borrow twenty until Friday?
  • She borrowed it from a friend and never gave it back.
The trap

✗ Can you borrow me twenty?  →  ✓ Can you lend me twenty? or ✓ Can I borrow twenty?

borrow never takes two objects. You borrow something, and if you name the other person it is always from them — never borrow me, borrow you, borrow him.

Same event, four ways — say all four out loud
  1. I lent her the money in March.
  2. She borrowed the money from me in March.
  3. She still owes me eight hundred of it.
  4. She paid me back last week — well, half of it.
Stage 03 of 04
Two conversations about money
Two roleplays. You play one side — I'll play the other. The first is a price to be argued; the second is a friendship with money sitting in the middle of it.
How it works

You only see your own brief. Every figure gets said out loud in English — no writing numbers down, no switching to Portuguese for the price. Each scene has six expressions attached to it — some from the toolkit, the rest in the sidebar on the right. Land all six before the scene ends.

Scenario A — The Car That's Almost Right Buyer vs. Private Seller
Advertised at R$48,000. You've driven forty minutes to see it. Saturday morning, on the street outside their building.
Land all six
What sort of figure are we talking? · Is there any movement on that? · a bit steep · a lot for what it is · knock (X) off · That's my limit, I'm afraid.
Your role — Buyer
  • The car is genuinely good, but the tyres are worn and there's a scratch along the door.
  • You have R$44,000. You could stretch to R$46,000 and that is a hard ceiling.
  • You want this car. That shouldn't be obvious in the first two minutes.
  • Two others are advertised cheaper — further away, higher mileage. Useful, and true.
  • Get a number out of them before you name one of your own.
Counterpart — Private seller
Hidden.
  • The money is needed by Friday — it's the deposit on a flat.
  • Two time-wasters have already been and gone this week.
  • R$43,500 is acceptable if genuinely pushed. Open by holding at R$48,000 and don't move for a while.
  • There's another viewing booked for this evening. True — though they cancelled once already. Mention it anyway.
Scenario B — The Money You Lent You vs. a Friend
R$1,200, lent four months ago. No date was ever agreed. You're having a coffee.
Land all six
money's tight · I'm a bit short · strapped for cash · I still owe you for… · Are we square? · Don't worry about it.
Your role — The one who lent it
  • You lent R$1,200 in March. It was urgent at the time and nobody mentioned paying it back.
  • Since then there's been a trip to Buenos Aires and a new pair of headphones. You noticed. You'd rather not say so.
  • Your car needs R$1,800 of work in two weeks. That's why it's suddenly a problem.
  • You want the money and you want the friendship. If it comes to choosing, choose the friendship.
  • Raise it in the first minute. Don't circle it for ten.
Counterpart — The friend
Hidden.
  • You remember R$400, not R$1,200 — you assumed the rest settled an older debt from years back.
  • Money is tight, but not desperate. Half now and half next month is doable.
  • Raised gently, you're apologetic and offer instalments straight away.
  • Made to sound like an accusation, you get defensive and bring up the weekend you paid for everything.
  • Don't volunteer the full figure until you're asked for it directly.
Within reach
Asking the price
What sort of figure are we talking? Is that your best price? What does that include? Anything on top of that?
Reacting
That's more than I had in mind. That seems steep for what it is. a lot for what it is How is it that much?
Moving it

Between friends

Wrap-up: what you can still say

Out loud, now
Ninety seconds. The best thing you've ever bought, and the worst.
  1. Give the actual price of each one, said out loud approximately.
  2. Put each one on the value scale and name the band before you explain it.
  3. Finish with the thing you'd splash out on again without thinking twice.
