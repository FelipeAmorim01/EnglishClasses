Stage 01 of 04
Warm-up
Describing people is where range shows most. You can talk through a project in real detail, but describe your brother-in-law and it comes out as nice, funny and a bit difficult — true of almost anyone. The words that actually get used for people are short, informal, and rarely in a textbook.
Let's talk
  • Think of a friend you've had for years. How did you first run into each other — and what did you make of them at the start?
  • Is there someone you never see eye to eye with, but still genuinely like? What makes it work?
  • Who's the most generous person you know, and who's the most penny-pinching? Has either of them ever surprised you?
  • How would your mother, your team and someone who's met you once each describe you? Who'd be closest to the truth?

Three of these rows are there because of Portuguese false friends. She's very simple means she isn't very bright — say down-to-earth. He's very educated means he went to university — say well-mannered. And sincere is far more formal than sincero — say direct.

Idioms
wear your heart on your sleeve
Show your feelings openly.
You always know what he's feeling. He wears his heart on his sleeve.
a chip on your shoulder
An old grudge about being treated unfairly, that colours everything.
She's got a real chip on her shoulder about not going to university.
rub (someone) up the wrong way
Irritate someone, often without meaning to.
I can't explain it — he just rubs me up the wrong way.
a dark horse
Someone with a hidden talent or a surprising past.
You play the piano? You're a dark horse.
the black sheep (of the family)
The one who doesn't fit what the family expected.
My uncle's the black sheep of the family — nobody's quite sure what he does for a living.
(her) bark is worse than (her) bite
Sounds much scarier than they really are.
Don't worry about the boss. Her bark's worse than her bite.
butter wouldn't melt (in his mouth)
Looks completely innocent — and isn't.
Look at him, sitting there like butter wouldn't melt, after what he said at dinner.
not suffer fools (gladly)
Have no patience with people who waste your time.
She doesn't suffer fools. Turn up unprepared and you'll know about it.
Phrasal verbs — getting on and falling out
come across (as)
Seem, to people who don't know you well.
He comes across as arrogant, but he's actually just shy.
hit it off
Like each other straight away.
We hit it off on the first day, and we've been friends for twenty years.
get on (with)
Have a good relationship.
I get on really well with my sister-in-law — better than my brother does.
fall out / make up
Argue and stop speaking / become friends again.
They fell out over money and didn't make up for ten years.
look up to
Admire and respect.
I always looked up to my grandfather — he built the business from nothing.
take after
Be like an older relative.
I take after my mum — same temper, same laugh.
open up
Start talking about personal things.
It took him months to open up about the divorce.
drift apart
Slowly stop being close.
We didn't fall out — we just drifted apart after university.
Language Tip
remember vs. remind
Portuguese runs both through one verb — eu lembro dele, ele me lembra meu pai, me lembra de ligar. English splits it in two. Remember happens inside your own head. Remind is when someone or something puts it into somebody's head — so it always needs a person straight after it.
remember — in your own head
I remember + someone / something
Remember to + verb
  • I remember him from university.
  • Do you remember her name?
  • Remember to call your mum.
remind — someone puts it in your head
X reminds me + of someone
Remind me + to + verb
  • He reminds me of my brother.
  • That song always reminds me of Rio.
  • Remind me to send that email.
The trap

✗ He remembers me my brother.  →  ✓ He reminds me of my brother.

✗ Remember me to call him.  →  ✓ Remind me to call him.

If a person comes straight after the verb, it's almost always remind. The one exception you'll hear is Remember me? — which means Don't you recognise me?

One old friend, both sides of the rule — say all four out loud
  1. I remembered his face straight away, but not his name.
  2. He had to remind me — Marcos, from the Lisbon office.
  3. His son reminds me of him at twenty — same laugh, same terrible jokes.
  4. Remind me to call him next week — and remember to save his number this time.

Wrap-up: what you can still say

Out loud, now
Ninety seconds. Describe someone you know well — someone I've never met — so clearly that I'd know exactly how a dinner with them would go.
  1. Put them on the scale first, and name the band.
  2. Three things about them, the way they actually get said — nothing from the textbook column.
  3. Who they take after, or who they remind you of.
  4. How you met — and whether you hit it off straight away, or they grew on you.
