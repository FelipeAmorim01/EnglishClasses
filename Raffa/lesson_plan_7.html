Stage 01 of 04
Warm-up
Health is where a fluent adult suddenly sounds like a textbook. You can run a meeting in English, but describe a pain to a pharmacist and it comes out as my head hurts and I feel bad. Most people only find that out on the day they need it — abroad, at a counter, feeling awful.
Let's talk
  • The last time you were properly ill — did it come out of the blue, or could you feel it building for days?
  • Have you ever been ill while living out of a suitcase on a work trip? Where did you end up getting help?
  • Are you a go-to-the-doctor-straight-away person, or do you put up with things until they go away on their own?
  • In Brazil the pharmacy is often the first stop, not the doctor. What's it like in the places you've travelled — and does it cost an arm and a leg?
  • Something health-related you're on the fence about — supplements, cold showers, sleep trackers, ten thousand steps a day. What would it take to convince you?

Idioms
on the mend
Getting better, but not there yet.
I'm on the mend — still coughing, but the worst is over.
back on your feet
Well enough to get on with normal life.
Give it a week and you'll be back on your feet.
(as) right as rain
Completely fine again.
I slept for fourteen hours and woke up as right as rain.
a clean bill of health
Officially told there's nothing wrong.
Had the full check-up — clean bill of health, apart from the coffee.
burn the candle at both ends
Work late and get up early until something gives.
Two months of burning the candle at both ends, and I got flu on my first day off.
run yourself into the ground
Exhaust yourself until you get ill.
You'll run yourself into the ground if you say yes to every trip they offer you.
sick as a dog
Very ill — usually with a stomach involved.
Something at the wedding buffet — I was sick as a dog all night.
a frog in your throat
A moment of hoarseness when you try to speak.
Sorry — frog in my throat. Give me a second.
Phrasal verbs
come down with
Start to get an illness.
I think I'm coming down with whatever the kids had last week.
pick up (a bug)
Catch something, usually without knowing where.
I must have picked something up on the plane.
go round
Spread through a group of people.
There's a stomach bug going round the office — half the team's off.
shake off
Get rid of an illness that keeps hanging on.
I've had this cough for three weeks and I just can't shake it off.
get over
Recover from.
It took me a month to get over that flu properly.
pass out
Faint, lose consciousness.
It was so hot in the queue that a guy in front of me passed out.
flare up
Come back suddenly, or suddenly get worse.
My back flares up every time I fly long-haul.
kick in / wear off
Start to work / stop working — medicine, mostly.
The painkillers took half an hour to kick in and wore off by four.
put (someone) on
Prescribe a course of something.
The doctor put me on antibiotics for a week.
Language Tip
be cold vs. have a cold
Portuguese runs all of these through one structure — estou com frio, estou com dor de cabeça, estou com febre. English splits them in two. How your whole body feels is an adjective, and takes be. Something you've caught, or something that hurts, is a noun, and takes have got — with an article in front of it.
be — how your body feels
I'm / I feel + adjective
cold · hot · hungry · dizzy · sick
  • I'm freezing — can we shut the window?
  • I felt dizzy and had to sit down.
  • I'm not hungry, I just feel a bit sick.
have got — something you've caught
I've got + a + noun
a cold · a headache · a temperature
  • I've got a cold, so I'll keep my distance.
  • She's had a headache since lunch.
  • He's got a temperature of thirty-nine.
The trap

✗ I'm with a headache.  →  ✓ I've got a headache.

✗ I have fever.  →  ✓ I've got a fever. or ✓ I'm running a temperature.

with never carries a symptom in English. And the article isn't decoration: I'm cold means shut the window; I've got a cold means keep your distance. The one exception worth knowing is flu — I've got the flu, or just I've got flu.

One bad night, both sides of the rule — say all four out loud
  1. I was freezing at midnight and boiling by two.
  2. I've got a cold — a proper one, with a temperature.
  3. I'm not hungry, I'm just exhausted and a bit dizzy.
  4. I've had a headache since I woke up, and now I'm cold again.

Wrap-up: what you can still say
Close the page first
Out loud, now
Sixty seconds. It's 7:30 in the morning and you're leaving a voicemail for your manager — you're not coming in today.
  1. Say what's wrong — two symptoms, the way they actually get said.
  2. Put it on the scale and name the band before you explain it.
  3. Say how it started, with a phrasal verb.
  4. Finish with when you expect to be back, without promising too much.
