Stage 01 of 04
Warm-up
Hypotheticals are where a conversation gets interesting — and where an answer in English tends to shrink to yes, I would or no, I wouldn't. Today is mostly talking: a short toolkit, then three rounds of what-would-you-do.
Let's talk
  • What's the biggest decision you've ever made without really thinking about it — and how did it turn out?
  • If money were no object, what's the first thing you'd spend it on?
  • If you could live in any city in the world for a year, which one would you pick?
  • Who do you look up to — and what would they do in your position right now?

The What-If Toolkit
A short one this week. Read the third column of the table out loud — each sentence gives you the pattern two or three times in one go.
What you'd say · what gets said
Correct What's actually said Read it out loud
Yes, I would do it immediately. I'd jump at the chance · in a heartbeat · without a second thought A year in Lisbon, all paid? I'd jump at the chance. I'd say yes in a heartbeat — without a second thought.
No, I would never do that. not in a million years · there's no way I'd… · you couldn't pay me (to) Skydiving? Not in a million years. There's no way I'd jump out of a plane — you couldn't pay me to.
That's an easy choice. a no-brainer · it's an easy one · it's not even close Beach or mountains? That's a no-brainer. The beach — it's an easy one. Honestly, it's not even close.
I can't decide. I'd be torn · I keep going back and forth · it's a tough call Stay or go? I'd be torn. I keep going back and forth on it, to be honest — it's a really tough call.
Maybe. I'm not sure. I'd be tempted · I'd think twice · I'd have to sleep on it Double the salary? I'd be tempted. But a six-day week — I'd think twice. I'd have to sleep on it.
Imagine that… say… · what if… · suppose… Say you lost your job tomorrow. What if nobody hired you for a year? Suppose you had to start again from zero — what would you do?
In your situation, I would… if I were you · in your shoes · if it were up to me If I were you, I'd take it. In your shoes, I wouldn't even hesitate — and if it were up to me, you'd have said yes already.
I regret it. looking back · if I'd known then what I know now · I'd do it differently Looking back, I'd do it differently. If I'd known then what I know now, I'd have moved abroad at twenty-five.

Almost everything in this table runs on 'd. Say it contracted — I'd, you'd, we'd. The full I would sounds like you're insisting.

Idioms & phrasal verbs — making the call
take a chance (on)
Try something risky that might not work out.
I took a chance on a tiny startup in 2015, and it really paid off.
get cold feet
Lose your nerve just before something big.
I had the contract in front of me, pen in hand — and got cold feet.
bite the bullet
Do the unpleasant thing you've been putting off.
In the end I just bit the bullet and told my boss I was leaving.
between a rock and a hard place
Every option is a bad one.
Lose the client or lose the team? I'm between a rock and a hard place.
the lesser of two evils
The option that's less bad.
Neither is great, but working Saturdays is the lesser of two evils.
play it safe
Choose the option with less risk.
I'd love to start my own business, but with two kids I'm playing it safe for now.
go for it
Take the chance — try it.
If they offer you the job, go for it. What's the worst that could happen?
think it over
Take time to think carefully before deciding.
I'm still thinking it over — the money's better, but the commute's awful.
turn down
Say no to an offer.
I turned down a job in London once. I still think about it.
miss out (on)
Lose the chance to do or have something good.
A free week in Japan? Go — you don't want to miss out.
settle for
Accept less than you wanted.
We wanted a house with a garden, but we settled for an apartment near the beach.
have second thoughts
Start to doubt a decision you've already made.
I signed the lease on Monday, and by Wednesday I was having second thoughts.
Language Tip
wish vs. hope
Portuguese pulls these together through desejar — desejo que você melhore comes out as I wish you get better. But in English, a full sentence after wish means it isn't true and isn't going to be. Hope is for things that could really happen. Wish is for things that can't — which is why it takes the same past form as if I were you.
hope — it could happen
I hope + present / will
  • I hope you get the job.
  • I hope it doesn't rain on Saturday.
  • I hope I'll see you there.
wish — it isn't true
I wish + past (had · could · were)
I wish + had done — for regrets
  • I wish I had more time.
  • I wish I could speak Italian.
  • I wish I'd said yes.
The trap

✗ I wish you get better soon.  →  ✓ I hope you get better soon.

✗ I hope I had more time.  →  ✓ I wish I had more time.

✗ I wish I can come.  →  ✓ I wish I could come.

The one forward-looking wish is with a noun, not a sentence: I wish you luck, I wish you all the best.

A friend's job interview, both sides of the rule — say all four out loud
  1. I hope you get it — honestly, you'd be brilliant.
  2. I wish I could help you prepare, but I'm away all week.
  3. I wish I'd had a chance like that at your age — I'd have jumped at it.
  4. Anyway, I wish you all the best — and I hope they call you back by Friday.

Round three — follow the thread
How it works

One big what-if, and five follow-ups, one at a time. Answer each before the next one appears — and keep your answers consistent all the way down. If you change your mind, say so: actually, I'm having second thoughts.
