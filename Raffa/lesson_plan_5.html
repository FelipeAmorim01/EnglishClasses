Stage 01 of 04
Warm-up: the volume you actually use
In Portuguese you have about twenty different ways to say "no, I don't think so" — and you pick the right one without thinking. In English most people have two: I agree and I disagree. Today we build the rest of the dial.
Let's talk
  • When did you last genuinely change your mind about something that mattered? What moved you — an argument, or just time?
  • Are you someone who says what they think straight away, or someone who reads the room first? Has that always been true, or did work teach it to you?
  • Long story short: what was your last real disagreement about — and did anyone actually move?
  • Is there an opinion you hold that most people around you don't? You don't have to defend it yet. Just say it.
  • What do you put up with in an argument, and what's the thing that makes you stop being polite?

The Disagreement Toolkit
Around seventy expressions. The centre of it is the ladder: five strengths of disagreement, five ways to say each.
Say it like a native speaker

The left column is correct English and nobody says it. Eight patterns that close the gap.

"In my opinion, the plan is bad." The way I see it, the plan doesn't work. The way I see it, we've got two options. And the way I see it, neither of them is great — but if you ask me, the second one is less bad.
"I think that is wrong." I'm not convinced, to be honest. I'm not convinced, to be honest. I'm not convinced the numbers are real, and I'm definitely not convinced we can do it by March.

The ladder: five strengths

Same disagreement, five volumes. Twenty-five expressions — read each rung out loud before moving on.

01 Hedged You're not really arguing. You're leaving the door open.
I mean, maybe. I'm not sure I'd go that far. I suppose so, up to a point. I can see it — I'm just not sure. Possibly. I'd want to look at it properly.
02 Doubtful Polite scepticism. You're signalling doubt without committing to a fight.
I'm not convinced, to be honest. I'd question that. I'd be surprised. I'm not sure that holds up. That doesn't quite ring true to me.
03 Clear You disagree and you say so. Still perfectly polite.
I see it differently. I don't think that's right. That's a bit of a stretch. I'd argue the opposite, actually. Honestly? I think that's wrong.
04 Firm You're not moving, and they can hear it.
I'm going to have to disagree there. That doesn't stand up. Sorry — I just don't buy that. With respect, that's not what happened. I'll be straight with you: no.
05 Flat The door is shut. Use it rarely, and mean it when you do.
No. That's not happening. That's simply not true. I'm not even going to entertain that. Absolutely not. That's a non-starter.
Idioms of the argument

Six. These are the ones that come up when a disagreement is being described rather than had — and every one of them will save you a whole sentence of explaining.

play devil's advocate
To argue a position you don't hold, to test it.
"Let me play devil's advocate for a second — what if the price isn't the problem?"
be on the fence
Undecided, genuinely. Not weak — often the honest answer.
"I'm still on the fence about the whole thing, to be honest."
that's beside the point
True, maybe, but irrelevant to what's being argued. Dismisses without calling anyone wrong.
"He was late, sure, but that's beside the point."
miss the point
To answer something other than what was actually said. Sharper than it looks — it's close to "you weren't listening".
"I think you're missing the point. Nobody's blaming the team."
agree to disagree
To stop arguing, on purpose, without either side giving in. Ends a conversation politely.
"I think we're just going to have to agree to disagree on this one."
talk past each other
Two people arguing about two different things without noticing.
"We spent forty minutes talking past each other."
The verbs an argument is made of

Twelve phrasal verbs. The single-word versions — oppose, concede, persuade, reject — are all correct and all sound like a legal document. These are what people say.

push back on
To resist or challenge something, without rejecting it outright. The most useful verb on this page.
"I pushed back on the deadline and they moved it a week."
back down
To stop insisting; to give up a position you were defending.
"He wouldn't back down, even when everyone else had moved on."
go along with
To accept something you don't really agree with, to keep the peace.
"I went along with it because nobody else objected."
come round to
To change your mind slowly and end up agreeing. Takes -ing or a noun.
"She's finally come round to the idea of moving."
stand by
To continue to support a decision or statement, especially under pressure.
"I stand by what I said in the meeting."
talk someone into / out of
To persuade someone to do something — or not to. Followed by -ing.
"They talked me into staying, and I talked them out of hiring him."
rule out
To eliminate an option completely. The opposite of leaving the door open.
"I'm not ruling it out, I just don't think it's the first thing to try."
water down
To weaken something — a proposal, a message — usually to make it acceptable to everyone.
"By the third draft it had been watered down to nothing."
pick holes in
To find and point out every small weakness in an argument. Slightly critical of the person doing it.
"He'll pick holes in it whatever you send him."
shoot down
To reject an idea quickly and publicly. Blunt, and it stings.
"My whole proposal got shot down in about ninety seconds."
get at
To mean or imply. Almost always in a question: what are you getting at?
"Sorry — what exactly are you getting at here?"
hold up
To survive examination. Used of arguments, evidence and excuses.
"That argument doesn't hold up if you look at last year."
Fixed expressions, by move

Grouped by what they do rather than what they mean.

Asking for the grounds
What makes you say that? Where are you getting that from? Says who, though? Hang on — how do you know that?
Conceding
Fair enough. OK, you've got a point. I hadn't thought of it that way.
Conceding, then turning
I take your point, but... I'm with you on the first bit, less so on the second. Even so, I'd still...
Agreeing emphatically
Couldn't agree more. Exactly — that's my whole point. You've hit the nail on the head. A hundred per cent.
Arguing a side you don't hold
Let me argue the other side for a second. Just to push on that — For the sake of argument, say you're right. I don't actually believe this, but...
Closing it down
I think we're going round in circles. We'll have to agree to disagree. Look — I'll leave it there.

The Strength Dial
One statement, five volumes. A statement appears, the dial gives you a number, and you respond at exactly that strength — no louder, no softer. The same opinion has to come out of your mouth five different ways before the next statement unlocks.
How it runs

Draw a statement. Roll the dial, or pick a rung. Answer at that exact strength using the language on the panel — then keep going until all five ticks are filled. Whoever isn't speaking pushes back once, so nobody gets to answer into silence.

Hit "New statement" to start. Nothing here is my opinion — argue with the sentence, not with me.

Nothing selected yet
Pick a rung above, or hit "Roll the dial" and take what you're given.

Two rules that make this work. First: no expression twice on the same statement — if you've used I'm not convinced once, it's gone. Second: rung 1 still has to be a real answer. "I don't know" isn't hedging, it's leaving.

Round two — you have to agree

Disagreeing is the easy half. Agreeing without saying "yes, I agree" is where most of the language above actually lives. Four positions — agree hard, using the agreeing emphatically bank right below, and then add something the other person didn't say.

Agreeing emphatically
Couldn't agree more. Exactly — that's my whole point. You've hit the nail on the head. A hundred per cent.
  • "Most meetings would be better with half the people in them."
  • "You learn more from a bad manager than a good one."
  • "Nobody has ever changed their mind because of an argument on the internet."
  • "The best decisions get made in the ten minutes after the meeting ends."
