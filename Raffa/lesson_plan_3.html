Stage 01 of 04
Warm-up: The floor is yours
Today you do most of the talking — so let's start as we mean to go on. Full answers, no single sentences.
Let's talk
  • When did you last have to present or explain something to a group? What was it, and how did it go?
  • What's worse for you — the presenting itself, or the questions afterwards? Why do you think that is?
  • Have you ever had to present in English? What happened to your English once the pressure was on?
  • Think of the best presenter you've seen at work. What exactly do they do that you don't?
  • If you had sixty seconds to make a stranger care about what your company does — go. I'm timing you.
Stage 02 of 04
The Pitch Toolkit
A pitch isn't a summary of everything you know. It's a recommendation, a reason, and an ask — said in language that sounds like you've already decided.
Say it like you mean it
Hesitant version Say this instead In context
"I think maybe we should..." My recommendation is... "My recommendation is that we rebuild the platform rather than patch it." Notice there's no maybe, no I think — you were asked for a recommendation, so give one.
"It's a good idea, I believe." The case for this is straightforward:... "The case for this is straightforward: we're losing two hours per order to manual work."
"The numbers are good." The numbers speak for themselves —... "The numbers speak for themselves — we're up 18% year on year with the same headcount."
"I don't know." (in Q&A) I don't have that figure to hand — let me come back to you. "I don't have that figure to hand, but I'll have it in your inbox this afternoon." Never invent a number in front of a room; promise it instead, then deliver.
"Sorry, this slide is a bit confusing." Let me walk you through this one. "Let me walk you through this one — the left half is today, the right half is twelve months from now." Never apologise for your own material.
"That's all. Thank you." So, what I need from you today is... "So, what I need from you today is a decision on the budget — ideally before the end of the week." Every pitch ends with an ask, not with thank you.
Signposting — telling them where they are
Moment Phrase Example
Opening the frame I'll keep this to ten minutes and leave time for questions. "I'll keep this to ten minutes and leave plenty of time for questions at the end."
Landing the point early Here's what I want you to take away. "Here's what I want you to take away: we don't have a sales problem, we have a delivery problem."
Moving on That brings me to... · Let's turn to... "That brings me to the part everyone asks about — cost."
Coming back Coming back to what I said about... "Coming back to what I said about churn — this is where it bites us."
Zooming out The bigger picture here is... "The bigger picture here is that our competitors solved this two years ago."
Closing So, to bring it all together... · If you remember one thing, make it this. "If you remember one thing today, make it this number: forty per cent."
Talking about numbers without sounding like a spreadsheet
What you mean How to say it Example
It went up / down a lot rose sharply · fell sharply · doubled · halved "Support tickets rose sharply after the June release."
It went up / down slowly rose steadily · edged up · dipped slightly "Revenue edged up through the second quarter."
It stopped changing levelled off · flattened out · flat year on year "Growth levelled off in March and has been flat since."
It got worse, then recovered bottomed out · rebounded · bounced back "It bottomed out in April and rebounded in June."
An approximate number roughly · just under · in the region of "We're talking about roughly two million a year."
A number you can't be held to a ballpark figure · give or take · broadly speaking "As a ballpark figure, six months — give or take a few weeks."
Idiom spotlight — boardroom edition
get buy-in
To get people to genuinely support a decision, not just approve it.
"Before the board meeting I got buy-in from both directors."
the bottom line
The final result, or the essential point.
"The bottom line is we can't ship this before December."
move the needle
To make a difference big enough to actually notice.
"Cutting travel saves money, but it won't move the needle."
low-hanging fruit
The easy wins you can take first, with little effort.
"Let's start with the low-hanging fruit and show results early."
cut to the chase
To skip the background and get to the important part.
"I'll cut to the chase — we need another three months."
make the business case
To argue for something in terms of cost, risk and return.
"I can make the business case, but I need your numbers first."
Stage 03 of 04
The Compression Ladder
One idea. Three pitches. Each one shorter than the last. You can cut anything you like — you can't add anything new.
How it works

Pick one real proposal from your job — something you actually want someone to say yes to. No real one to hand? Use the system rebuild from stage 2, a hire you want signed off, or a process you'd change tomorrow if it were up to you. I'll run the clock. When it hits zero you stop, even mid-sentence — and whatever you hadn't said yet, you'd never have said in the real meeting either.

Useful language
Opening strong
I'll get straight to the point. My recommendation is... Here's the problem in one sentence.
The one-sentence version
In one sentence: ... The short version is... If we do nothing, ... It comes down to one number.
Cutting without apologising
I'll skip the background. The detail's in the deck. Two things matter here. I'll spare you the history.
Closing / the ask
What I need from you today is... Can I put you down for...? If you take one thing away, make it this.
Stage 04 of 04
Reflection & Next Steps
Let's close with these questions
  • Which round was hardest — and which one produced your single best sentence? They're rarely the same round.
  • Look back at the grid. What survived to ten seconds? Is that what you'd have predicted before we started?
  • What did you cut that you're still tempted to put back? Be honest about what that part was really doing for you.
