Which one, and why?

The conversation this whole lesson is for, and you have it for real — nothing here is for repeating. I give you a choice from the first row. You answer by finishing one of the second row with something true, always with a reason, and keep it going with the third. After I'd rather, the verb comes with no to. Then we swap: you give me a choice. My first answer will be It depends — get a real choice and a reason out of me before you close it.

1 · I give you a choice
Which do you prefer, … or …? Would you rather … or …? Which is better for you, … or …? What's the best … in your city?
2 · You answer — finish one with something true
I prefer … to …, because it's … I'd rather … than … For me, … is much better, because … … isn't as … as …, but … Both are good, but … is a bit … It depends. If …, I prefer …
3 · You keep it going
What about you? Really? Why? Is it really that much better? So which one is the best, for you? I see what you mean, but …
4 · Swap — you ask, then close it
Fair enough. Good point. I think you're right. Let's agree to disagree!
Stage 02 of 04
Your comparing toolkit
Four blocks, easiest first. Each one is a way to choose between things.
1 · -er and more … than

Comparing two things. Short words take -er on the end; long words take more in front. Then than — every time. less goes the other way, and works with any long word. The spelling sometimes moves a little: big → bigger, busy → busier. I read the first row of the table, then the italic sentences are yours; the boxed words are mine first and yours after, twice each; green lines are yours, twice each.

The word Comparing two Say it out loud
cheap cheaper than The bus is cheaper than the metro, and the metro is cheaper than a taxi.
big bigger than My new office is bigger than the old one, and the kitchen is much bigger.
busy busier than Mondays are busier than Fridays, and this Monday is busier than ever.
expensive more expensive than Rent in the center is more expensive than rent out here — far more expensive.
stressful less stressful than Working from home is less stressful than the commute, and a lot less tiring.
good · bad better · worse than The new system is better than the old one, but the coffee is worse than ever.
How big is the difference?
a bit cheaper slightly faster much bigger a lot better far more expensive even worse
It's bigger that my old one.
It's bigger than my old one.
than, every time you compare. that is a different word.
It's very cheaper.
It's much cheaper.
very never goes before -er or more. Use much, a lot, far or a bit.
The train is more easy.
The train is easier.
Two syllables ending in -y count as short: easier, busier, happier.
2 · The best, the worst, the most …

More than all the others — one winner. Short words take the -est, long words take the most, and the always comes first. After it: in for a place or a group, of for a time or a number, and I've ever … for your whole life — learn that last ending whole: I've ever had, seen, been to. I read the first row, then the italic sentences are yours; green lines are yours, twice each.

The word The winner Say it out loud
cheap the cheapest This is the cheapest café in the area, and the coffee is the best.
busy the busiest Friday is the busiest day of the week, and December is the busiest month of the year.
expensive the most expensive It's the most expensive city in Brazil, and one of the most exciting.
good the best That was the best meal I've ever had — the best of the whole trip.
bad the worst This is the worst traffic I've ever seen, and the worst part is that it's Sunday.
far the furthest It's the furthest office from my house, and the hardest one to get to.
It's the more expensive in the city.
It's the most expensive in the city.
Two things: more. One winner out of all of them: the most.
It's the best of the world.
It's the best in the world.
A place or a group takes in: in the world, in the city, in my team.
It was the worse day of my week.
It was the worst day of my week.
worse is for two; the worst is the winner. The t at the end is the only sound between them — say it.
3 · not as … as, the same as, different from

The softer way to compare, and the way to say two things are equal. Both sides take as — never than. It often sounds kinder: not as big as is gentler than smaller. I read the first row, then the italic sentences are yours; green lines are yours, twice each.

Pattern Example Say it out loud
not as … as The bus isn't as fast as the metro. The bus isn't as fast as the metro, and it isn't as comfortable.
just as … as My new job is just as interesting as the old one. My new job is just as interesting as the old one, and just as busy.
twice as … as That apartment is twice as expensive as mine. That apartment is twice as expensive as mine, but it isn't twice as big.
as … as possible Send it as soon as possible. Send it as soon as possible, and keep it as short as possible.
the same as My salary is the same as last year. My salary is the same as last year, but my rent is much higher.
different from · similar to Recife is very different from São Paulo. Recife is very different from São Paulo, but it's quite similar to Salvador.
It's as big than mine.
It's as big as mine.
as on both sides. than only comes after -er or more.
It's very different of the old one.
It's very different from the old one.
different from, similar to, the same as — each one has its own small word. Learn the three pairs whole.
My phone is the same that yours.
My phone is the same as yours.
the same as, every time — never the same that.
4 · Words to compare with

Words for the two things people compare most: jobs, and places to live. They come in opposite pairs, so learn them two at a time. Most are long and take more — more stressful, more flexible. The short ones take -er — safer, quieter, emptier. One breaks the rule: well-paid → better-paid → the best-paid. Boxed pairs are mine first, then yours, twice each; green lines are yours, twice each.

A job
well-paid · badly paid stressful · relaxed flexible · strict stable · risky rewarding · boring demanding · easy
A place to live
lively · quiet crowded · empty safe · dangerous spacious · cramped affordable · overpriced convenient · out of the way
The trip was very funny.
The trip was really fun.
fun is a good time; funny makes you laugh. And it's more fun, the most fun — never funner.
The life in São Paulo is more expensive.
Life in São Paulo is more expensive.
No the when you mean it in general: life, rent, traffic, people.
I prefer the bus than the metro.
I prefer the bus to the metro.
prefer … to …. It's than only with I'd rather: I'd rather walk than drive.
Language Tip
cheaper vs. more expensive
Every comparison on this page makes the same choice first: does the word take -er, or does more go in front of it? The word decides, not you — and it turns on how long the word is. Get that choice right and the winner comes with it: -er goes with the -est, more goes with the most.
short words — one syllable, or two ending in -y
word + -er · the + word + -est
  • The bus is cheaper than the metro.
  • Fridays are busier than Mondays.
  • It's the easiest way to get there.
long words — two syllables or more
more + word · the most + word
  • Rent is more expensive in the center.
  • My job is more flexible than yours.
  • It's the most stressful part of the day.
The trap

✗ It's more cheaper than the bus.  →  ✓ It's cheaper than the bus.  ·  ✗ It's the most cheapest.  →  ✓ It's the cheapest.

-er and more do the same job — use one, never both. The same goes for better and worse: they already mean more good and more bad, so there is never a more better. Three words do their own thing, and are worth learning whole: good → better → the best, bad → worse → the worst, far → further → the furthest.

Both sides — say all four out loud
  1. The metro is faster than the bus, but it's more expensive.
  2. This is the cheapest apartment in the building, but not the most comfortable.
  3. My new job is more flexible, and the people are nicer.
  4. Traffic is getting worse, and parking is getting more expensive.

One short scene

You play yourself, with two job offers and five days to choose. I play a friend who wants to know which one you'll take — and who keeps asking why. The two offers and your half are below; you get a minute to read them.

Two offers You vs. a friend who keeps asking why
Sunday evening. Both companies want an answer by Friday. This is what each one is offering.
Offer A — a big company in the center
  • R$ 15,000 a month.
  • An hour and ten minutes each way, by metro and then bus.
  • In the office five days a week, 9:00 to 18:00.
  • A team of forty. The company is big, well-known and fifty years old.
  • 30 days off a year, and an English course paid for.
Offer B — a small company near home
  • R$ 12,500 a month, and a bonus if the year goes well.
  • Twenty minutes by car — and only two days a week. The rest is from home.
  • Start any time between 8:00 and 10:00.
  • A team of eight. The company is young, busy and only two years old.
  • 20 days off a year.
You — with two offers
  • Compare them out loud: one -er, one more, and one with much or far.
  • Say the best thing and the worst thing about each one.
  • Use not as … as at least once.
  • Choose one, and close it in one sentence: I'm taking … because …
Me — a friend who keeps asking why
Hidden until the scene starts.
  • "So, which one is better?"
  • Answers every reason with "Really? Why?"
  • Thinks money is the most important thing — and says so.
  • "Isn't a small company much riskier?"
  • Halfway through: Offer B calls back. They can pay R$ 14,500 now — but they want you in the office three days a week, not two.
