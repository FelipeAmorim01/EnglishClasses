What's new?

The conversation this whole lesson is for, and you have it for real — nothing here is for repeating. I ask you what's new, from the first row. You answer by finishing one of the second row with something true, and keep it going with the third.

1 · I ask you
So, what's new with you? What have you done this week? Have you done anything for the first time this year?
2 · You answer — finish one with something true
Not much — I've just … I've been really busy with … since … I haven't … yet, but I'm going to … I've finally …, and it was …
3 · You keep it going
What about you? Have you …? Have you ever …? How long have you …? How did it go?
Stage 02 of 04
Your experience toolkit
Four blocks, easiest first: things you've done in your life, news at work, how long, and the verbs that go with the words.
1 · Have you ever …? I've never … — your life up to now

have or has + the third form of the verb: I've been, she's seen. Use it for your life up to now, with no time in the sentence. been to means you went and came back. The third forms are listen and repeat only — several don't sound the way they look: done rhymes with sun.

What you're saying The words Say it out loud
asking Have you ever …? Have you ever been to Chile? Have you ever eaten sushi?
yes Yes, I have. Yes, I have — I've been there twice. I've been there for work.
no I've never … No, I haven't. I've never been there, and I've never eaten sushi.
someone else She's … · He's never … She's lived in three countries. He's never lived abroad.
how many times once · twice · three times I've been to Rio once, to Salvador twice, and to Recife three times.
the details then the past I've been to Chile once. I went in 2018, and I loved it.
Third forms — the same as the past
work → worked have → had make → made meet → met buy → bought lose → lost
Third forms — a new one
be → been do → done see → seen eat → eaten take → taken write → written
Have you ever been in London?
Have you ever been to London?
For a trip, it's been to: you went there and came back. been in is where you still are: I've been in this meeting since nine.
Have you ever went to Chile?
Have you ever been to Chile?
After have, the third form — been, seen, done. Never the past: went, saw, did.
I never been to Japan.
I've never been to Japan.
The have can't go — it's what makes the sentence work. never goes after it: I've never been, she's never seen.
2 · just, already, yet, still — news at work

For news: something done recently that matters now. just is a moment ago, already is sooner than expected — both go between have and the verb. yet goes at the very end, in questions and negatives. still is for something late, and goes before haven't.

What you're saying The words Say it out loud
a moment ago I've just … She's just left — you've just missed her. I've just sent it.
sooner than expected I've already … I've already finished the report. Have you already had lunch?
asking … yet? Have you heard back yet? Has she replied yet?
not done I haven't … yet I haven't called him yet. We haven't decided yet.
late I still haven't … I still haven't heard back. They still haven't paid us.
the details then the past I've already sent it — I sent it at nine, before the meeting.
I've finished already the report.
I've already finished the report.
already and just go between have and the verb, the same place as never.
I haven't finished still.
I haven't finished yet.
In a negative, the end of the sentence takes yet. still moves to the front: I still haven't finished.
Has she replied? — Yes, she already.
Has she replied? — Yes, she has.
The short answer keeps have or has: Yes, I have. No, not yet.
3 · for and since — how long

Something that started in the past and is still true now takes have + the third form: I've worked here for six years. for + how long (six years, a month). since + when it started (2020, March, I was a kid). For an activity that's still going on: I've been …ing.

What you're saying The words Say it out loud
asking How long have you …? How long have you worked here? How long have you lived in São Paulo?
how long for … I've worked here for six years. I've had this car for a month.
when it started since … I've lived here since 2020, and I've known Ana since we were kids.
not for a while I haven't … since · for I haven't seen him since March. I haven't had a holiday for two years.
still going on I've been …ing I've been learning English since September. I've been waiting for twenty minutes.
it's over then the past I worked in a bank for five years, but I left in 2019.
I work here since 2020.
I've worked here since 2020.
Still true, and you say since when: have + the third form. I work here is fine on its own — just not with for or since.
I've lived here since two years.
I've lived here for two years.
for is how long, since is when it started: for two years, since 2024.
How much time do you work here?
How long have you worked here?
Ask with How long, and with the same have + third form as the answer.
4 · made, taken, missed — the verbs that go with the words

Some words choose their verb, and the choice isn't logical — it's just what English says. You make a mistake, take a course, miss a bus and spend a weekend. Learn the verb together with the word.

What it's about The words Say it out loud
an error make a mistake Sorry, I've made a mistake. I've made the same mistake twice!
choosing make a decision Have you made a decision yet? We haven't made a decision yet.
studying take a course I've taken two online courses. Have you ever taken a course in English?
a film, a series watch · see I've watched that series twice. Have you seen the new film yet?
too late miss the bus · miss a call Sorry, I've missed your call. I've missed the bus again!
time spend the weekend · spend time I've spent the whole weekend at home. We've spent too much time on this.
I've done a mistake in the report.
I've made a mistake in the report.
make a mistake, make a decision. do is for work and tasks: do the report, do your homework.
I've made an English course.
I've taken an English course.
A course is taken. You only make one if you're the teacher who wrote it.
Have you assisted the new series?
Have you watched the new series?
assist means help: Can I assist you? A film, a game or a series is watched.
Language Tip
I've been vs. I went
Both talk about the past — the difference is the time. When the time is finished and you say it — yesterday, in 2019, last week, two years ago — use the past. When there's no time, or the time isn't over yet — your life, this week, since 2020 — use have + the third form. So ask yourself: is there a finished time in the sentence?
I've been — up to now, no finished time
have / has + the third form
  • I've been to Chile twice.
  • Have you finished the report yet?
  • I've worked here since 2020.
I went — a finished time
the past + a finished time
  • I went to Chile in 2018.
  • I finished the report yesterday.
  • When did you start here? — In 2020.
The trap

✗ I have visited Paris in 2019.  →  ✓ I visited Paris in 2019.

2019 is over, and it's in the sentence — so it takes the past. Take the time out and have comes back: I've visited Paris. The same goes for When: it always asks for a finished time, so it always takes the past — When did you go?, never When have you been?

Both sides — say all four out loud
  1. I've been to Chile twice — the last time was in 2023.
  2. Have you sent the email yet? — Yes, I sent it an hour ago.
  3. I've worked here since 2020, but I worked in a bank before that.
  4. I've just seen Ana — she arrived ten minutes ago.

Two truths and a lie
You first, then me

Tell me three things you've done in your life. Two are true and one isn't. I ask questions to find the lie, and you answer every one — true or not. Then I tell you my three, and you find mine.

1 · Your three
Three sentences with I've …, I've never … or I've … for … — no times in them yet.
2 · My questions
I ask for the details — when, who with, what it was like. Your answers are in the past, and the lie needs details too.
3 · My three
Now I tell you three. Ask me at least two questions about each one.
4 · Your guess
Say which one is the lie, and why: I think you've never …, because …
Keep these in view
Saying it
I've been to … I've never … I've … for … years. I've … since …
Asking for details
When did you …? Who did you go with? What was it like? How long did you stay?
Guessing
No way — really? I don't believe you. I think that's the lie.
If you get stuck
Sorry, I don't understand. Can you say that again? Let me think. How do you say this in English?
