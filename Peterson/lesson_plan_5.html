How was your week at work?

The conversation this whole lesson is for, and you have it for real — nothing here is for repeating. I ask you about your week, from the first row. You answer by finishing one of the second row with something true, and keep it going with the third.

1 · I ask you
What was the hardest thing you did this week? Did you manage to finish everything? Was there anything you couldn't do? Did anyone help you? What would you like to be able to do better?
2 · You answer — finish one with something true
I had to …, and it wasn't easy, because … I managed to …, but I couldn't … In the end I was able to …, because … I didn't manage to …, so … I'm hoping to be able to … by …
3 · You keep it going
What about you? How was your week? Did you manage to …? How did you do it? Who did you ask for help?
Stage 02 of 04
Your asking toolkit
Four blocks, easiest first: what you managed to do, what's probably true, and how to ask for things without sounding rude.
1 · could, managed to, be able to

can only has two forms — can and could — so everywhere else it borrows be able to. In the past there's a catch: could is for something you could do in general, over a long time. For one hard thing that you did anyway, say managed to or was able to. In a negative, couldn't works for both.

When The words Say it out loud
in general, in the past could When I was twenty, I could run ten kilometers, and I could stay up all night.
one hard thing — you did it managed to The traffic was terrible, but I managed to get there on time. I even managed to park.
one hard thing — the same was able to The system was down, but I was able to send it from my phone, and I was able to call the client.
you didn't couldn't · didn't manage to I couldn't find the file, and I didn't manage to finish the report.
the future will be able to I won't be able to come on Friday, but I'll be able to join on Monday.
after another verb to be able to I'd like to be able to present in English, and I hope to be able to do it this year.
I could finish the report yesterday.
I managed to finish the report yesterday.
One hard thing, done on one day, isn't could. Say managed to or was able to. The negative is fine: I couldn't finish it.
I will can come on Monday.
I'll be able to come on Monday.
can never comes after another verb — will, might, want to. be able to takes its place.
I managed finish it on time.
I managed to finish it on time.
manage always takes to: managed to send, managed to call, didn't manage to park.
2 · must, can't, might — making a guess

When you don't know, but you can guess from what you see. must — you're almost sure it's true. can't — you're almost sure it isn't. might or could — maybe. The verb after them stays as it is: must be, might know, can't be working.

How sure The words Say it out loud
almost sure — yes must He isn't answering — he must be in a meeting. He must be really busy.
almost sure — no can't That can't be right — it can't be five o'clock already.
maybe might She might be stuck in traffic, or she might be on the metro.
maybe could It could be the Wi-Fi, or it could be your laptop.
maybe not might not He might not know about the meeting, and he might not have the address.
reacting to someone You must be … You must be tired! You must be happy it's over.
He can be in a meeting.
He might be in a meeting.
For a guess about now, use might or could. can is for what's possible in general: It can be cold in July.
That mustn't be true.
That can't be true.
The opposite of a must guess is can't. mustn't means it's against the rules — see the next block.
She must to be at home.
She must be at home.
must goes straight to the verb, like can.
3 · Would it be OK if I …? — permission and rules

The bigger the favor, the longer the question. Can I …? for small things; Would it be OK if I …? and Would you mind if I …? for bigger ones — and after would … if I, the verb goes into the past, even though you're asking about the future. For rules: don't have to means it isn't necessary; not allowed to and mustn't mean it's against the rules.

How big Ask Say it out loud
small Can I …? Can I borrow your charger? Can I give it back after lunch?
bigger Is it OK if I …? Is it OK if I work from home tomorrow? Is it OK if I start at ten?
big Would it be OK if I left …? Would it be OK if I left at two on Friday? Would it be OK if I took Monday off?
big Would you mind if I opened …? Would you mind if I opened the window? — No, not at all. Go ahead.
very big Is there any chance I could …? Is there any chance I could have a new laptop? Is there any chance I could have it this week?
the rules don't have to · not allowed to You don't have to wear a suit, but you're not allowed to wear shorts.
Would it be OK if I leave early on Friday?
Would it be OK if I left early on Friday?
After would … if I, the verb goes into the past. It doesn't mean the past — it makes the question softer.
You mustn't come to the meeting — it's optional.
You don't have to come to the meeting — it's optional.
don't have to: you can if you want. mustn't: you're not allowed to.
Do you mind if I sit here? — Yes, sit down!
Do you mind if I sit here? — No, go ahead!
Do you mind …? asks if it's a problem for you. Yes means it is a problem — so to say OK, say No.
4 · I was wondering if you could … — asking, and saying no

At work, the softer the request, the more likely the yes. I was wondering if you could … and Would you be able to …? are the softest ways to ask. To ask for information politely, put the question inside Could you tell me …? or Do you know …? And to say no, give a reason and something else instead.

What you're doing The words Say it out loud
asking, softly I was wondering if you could … I was wondering if you could take a look at my slides — and if you could do it before Thursday.
asking, softly Would you be able to …? Would you be able to send me the numbers? Would you be able to send them by three?
asking for information Could you tell me …? Could you tell me where the meeting is, and what time it starts?
asking for information Do you know if …? Do you know if the client is coming? Do you know if she's bringing her team?
saying no I'd love to, but … I'd love to, but I'm afraid I won't be able to — I'm in Brasília all week.
offering something else I can't …, but I could … I can't do it today, but I could do it first thing tomorrow.
I was wondering if you can help me.
I was wondering if you could help me.
was wondering is in the past, so can moves back to could — and it sounds softer.
Do you know is he coming?
Do you know if he's coming?
A yes/no question inside another question needs if.
No, I can't.
I'm afraid I can't — I'm on a call until four.
A bare no sounds rude at work. Add I'm afraid, a reason — and, if you can, something else instead.
Language Tip
Where is it? vs. Could you tell me where it is?
A normal question flips the order: the verb jumps in front — Where is the meeting? — or do comes in to help — What time does it start? A polite question wraps that question inside a frame like Could you tell me …? or Do you know …? The frame already does the asking, so the question inside goes back to the order of a normal sentence: no flip, no do.
a normal question — it flips
where + is + the meeting?
  • Where is the meeting?
  • What time does it start?
  • Is the client coming?
inside a frame — normal order
Could you tell me + where + the meeting is?
  • Could you tell me where the meeting is?
  • Do you know what time it starts?
  • Do you know if the client is coming?
The trap

✗ Do you know where is the printer?  →  ✓ Do you know where the printer is?

Once the frame has asked, the question inside is just information. So it goes back to normal order — the person or thing, then the verb: the printer is, it starts. The do goes when the flip goes: What time does it start? becomes Do you know what time it starts? A yes/no question has no question word, so it borrows if: Is he coming? becomes Do you know if he's coming? I was wondering if you could … works the same way.

Both sides — say all four out loud
  1. Where's the printer? — Do you know where the printer is?
  2. What time does the meeting start? — Could you tell me what time it starts?
  3. Is the client coming? — Do you know if the client is coming?
  4. Can you help me? — I was wondering if you could help me.

Four favors
You and me, at work

I'm your manager, and I'm very busy. You need four things from me this week — ask for each one as softly as you can. I'll say yes to two and no to two. When I say no, find another way: ask for something smaller, offer something back, or change the time.

1 · The numbers
You need last month's sales numbers from me before your meeting at three today.
2 · Friday
You want to leave at two on Friday. You've got a flight at five.
3 · Your slides
You want me to look at your slides before your presentation on Thursday.
4 · Your laptop
Yours is very slow, and you want a new one this week.
