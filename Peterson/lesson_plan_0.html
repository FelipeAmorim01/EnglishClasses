1 · Staying in English

This class runs in English, which only works if these sentences come out automatically instead of silence. I say each one first, you say it back.

When you don't understand
Sorry? Sorry, I don't understand. I don't know this word. What does "busy" mean? I understand a little.
Asking for it again
Can you repeat that, please? Can you say that again? Can you say it slowly, please? One more time, please.
Asking for a word
How do you say this in English? What's this in English? How do you spell that? Can you write it, please?
Buying yourself a second
Just a moment. Let me think. How do I say this…
Checking
Is this correct? Like this? Did I say it right?
2 · am / is / are

The short forms in the middle column are what people actually say. Column three is a sentence to read out loud.

Full form Say this Read it out loud
I am from Brazil. I'm from Brazil. I'm Peterson. I'm from Brazil, and I'm very happy to be here.
You are late. You're late. You're late again. You're always late — but you're a good friend, so it's fine.
He is my manager. He's my manager. He's my manager. He's from Recife. He's not in the office today.
She is not here. She isn't here. She isn't here today. She's at home. She's sick, but she isn't very sick.
We are in a meeting. We're in a meeting. We're in a meeting now. We're on the fourth floor. We're finished at three.
They are not ready. They aren't ready. They aren't ready. They're still working, and they aren't happy about it.
Asking — and the short answer
Are you Brazilian? — Yes, I am. Are you tired? — No, I'm not. Is he your manager? — Yes, he is. Is she here? — No, she isn't. Are they new? — Yes, they are.
3 · Saying who you are

These run the other way round: I ask the question, you answer it. The line underneath is one good answer, not the only one — change the details to make it true.

What's your name?
My name is… / I'm…
"My name is Peterson. Just Peterson."
Where are you from?
I'm from + country or city
"I'm from Brazil — from a small town near Belo Horizonte."
Where do you live?
I live in + city
"I live in São Paulo, in an apartment near the center."
How old are you?
I'm + number (+ years old)
"I'm thirty-four." / "I'm thirty-four years old."
Do you speak English?
A little. / I'm learning.
"A little. I'm learning. I understand more than I speak."
Are you married?
I'm married. / I'm single.
"I'm married. I have two children — a boy and a girl."
How do you spell that?
Letter by letter, slowly
"P-E-T-E-R-S-O-N. Peterson."
What's your phone number?
Say the digits one by one
"It's nine one — nine eight seven six — five four three two."
Nine sentences to unlearn

These nine come out of a Portuguese speaker's mouth almost every time. Say the green sentence once.

I have thirty-four years.
I'm thirty-four.
Age uses am, never have.
I have hungry.
I'm hungry.
Same with: I'm thirsty · I'm cold · I'm hot · I'm afraid.
I have sure.
I'm sure.
And: I'm right · I'm wrong · I'm lucky.
I am agree.
I agree.
Agree is already the verb — it doesn't need am.
I'm with sleep.
I'm tired. / I'm sleepy.
Nothing in English is "with" a feeling.
I have five minutes late.
I'm five minutes late.
And: I'm early · I'm on time.
Yes, I like very much.
Yes, I like it very much.
Like always needs something after it — it, them, this job.
Actually, I work at a bank.
Right now, I work at a bank.
Actually means "in fact", not "now". Use right now or at the moment.
I pretend to travel in June.
I plan to travel in June.
Pretend means to act, to fake. The word you want is plan or intend.
4 · Work
The question Say this Read it out loud
What do you do? I'm a designer. I'm a designer. I work at a small agency in São Paulo, and I work with two other designers.
Where do you work? I work for a bank. I work for a bank. I work in the finance department. I work on the tenth floor.
Who do you work with? I work with a small team. I work with a small team. My manager is very good, my colleagues are nice, and my team is only five people.
Do you like your job? Yes, I do. / It's OK. Yes, I do. I like my team, I like the work, and I don't like the hours.
I'm engineer.
I'm an engineer.
A job always takes a or an. Use an before a vowel sound: an engineer, an accountant, an architect, an IT manager.

5 · Your day

The block where nearly everyone loses the -s. He, she and it take an extra s — and then, in a negative or a question, that s jumps onto does and the verb goes back to normal.

The form Say this Read it out loud
I / you / we / they + verb I start work at eight. I start work at eight. I check my email, I answer the important ones, and I start the real work at nine.
he / she / it + verb + s She starts at nine. She starts at nine. She works from home on Fridays, and she finishes early on Fridays too.
don't / doesn't + verb I don't work on Sundays. I don't work on Sundays. I don't check my email, and my manager doesn't call me.
Do / Does + you + verb? Do you work on Saturdays? Do you work on Saturdays? Do you work from home? Does your team work from home too?
She work in marketing.
She works in marketing.
He, she and it always add the s.
She doesn't works here.
She doesn't work here.
Only one s per sentence — does already has it.

The second list is in order on purpose. Say it as a sequence — your own day, start to finish — instead of reading it as words.

6 · Yesterday

Every box in this block is listen-and-repeat, with no exceptions. Nothing here can be guessed from the spelling — thought, said and took least of all. And the + ed ending has three different sounds: worked and talked finish with a t sound, called with a d, wanted with id.

The Question Machine
Answer the question, then ask the same question back. Every card you take is a card nobody gets twice.
Draw a question
Nothing drawn yet. Take the button, and take whatever comes out.

Answer in a full sentence, never one word. Then ask it back — that second half is where the question forms get practised.

Build a sentence
Pick positive, negative or question, then press Build. Say the sentence out loud before you press it again.
Make it

The machine writes it; your job is to say it. When one comes out easily, say it again and change one word — the subject, the time, the place.

Keep these in view
Answering
Yes, I do. No, I don't. Yes, I am. No, I'm not. Sometimes. Not really.
Asking it back
And you? What about you? How about you? Do you? Are you?
Getting more
Why? Really? Tell me more. That's interesting. Me too. Me neither.
If you get stuck
Sorry, I don't understand. Can you say that again? Let me think. How do you say this in English?
One short scene

You play the new employee — it is your first week and you have met almost nobody. I play the colleague. Your half is on the left and you get a minute to prepare it; my half stays hidden until we start, because in real life it would be.

The kitchen at work New employee vs. a colleague you've never met
Nine in the morning, your first week in the job. You are in the office kitchen making a coffee. Someone walks in, sees you, and says hello.
You — the new employee
  • Say hello. It is nine in the morning.
  • Say your name, and spell it.
  • Say your job and which team you are on.
  • Say it is your first week.
  • Ask two questions back.
  • Say goodbye — you have a meeting at half past nine.
Me — a colleague from another team
Hidden until the scene starts.
  • Warm, friendly, and speaks a little too fast.
  • "Morning! You're new here, right? I'm Sam."
  • "Sorry — how do you spell that?"
  • "Nice one. So what do you do here?"
  • One line said quickly and quietly, so the repeat phrases have to come out.
