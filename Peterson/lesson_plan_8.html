Eating out

The conversation this whole lesson is for, and you have it for real — nothing here is for repeating. I ask you from the first row. You answer by finishing one of the second row with something true, and keep it going with the third.

1 · I ask you
Where do you like to eat out? What did you have the last time you ate out? Have you ever sent food back in a restaurant?
2 · You answer — finish one with something true
There's a great … place near … I usually have the …, because … The last time I ate out, I had …, and it was … I've never sent anything back, but once …
3 · You keep it going
What about you? Where do you like to eat? Have you ever been to …? What would you recommend there? Is it expensive?
Stage 02 of 04
Your ordering toolkit
Four blocks, easiest first: ordering, too and enough, sending something back, and shopping.
1 · I'll have, could I get — ordering

To order, I'll have … or Could I get …? — both polite, both normal. To ask about a dish: what it comes with, and the polite question from lesson 5, Do you know if …? At the end it's the check — in the UK, the bill.

What you're saying The words Say it out loud
arriving a table for two Hi, a table for two, please. Could we have a table by the window?
choosing I'll have … · Could I get …? I'll have the steak, please. Could I get a glass of water too?
what's on the plate What does it come with? What does the fish come with? Does it come with rice or fries?
what's in it Do you know if it has …? Do you know if it has nuts? Do you know if the soup has meat in it?
your way without … · on the side Could I have it without onions? Could I get the sauce on the side?
the end Could we get the check? Could we get the check, please? Can we pay at the table?
Can you bring me the account?
Could we get the check, please?
An account is at the bank. In a restaurant it's the check — or the bill in the UK.
I want the fish.
I'll have the fish, please.
I want sounds rude to a waiter. Order with I'll have or Could I get.
Is it with rice?
Does it come with rice?
What's on the plate with it comes with it: The steak comes with fries and a salad.
2 · too salty, not hot enough — too and enough

too + a describing word is more than you want — it's a problem: too salty, too expensive. not … enough is less than you need: not hot enough. enough goes after a describing word, but before a thing: big enough, enough time. Add to + a verb for what you can't do: too hot to eat.

What you're saying The words Say it out loud
more than you want too … It's too salty, and the music is too loud.
for one person too … for me It's too spicy for me. These shoes are too small for me.
so you can't too … to … It's too hot to eat right now. It's too late to order.
less than you need not … enough The soup isn't hot enough. The table isn't big enough for six.
just right … enough Is it big enough? — Yes, it's big enough for all of us.
a thing enough + a thing We don't have enough time. Is there enough food for everyone?
It's too much expensive.
It's too expensive.
A describing word takes too on its own. too much is for a thing: too much salt, too much noise.
The table isn't enough big.
The table isn't big enough.
enough comes after the describing word: big enough, hot enough, good enough.
It's very hot to eat.
It's too hot to eat.
very is just a lot — It's very hot is fine. When you can't do something because of it, it's too.
3 · I'm afraid this isn't what I ordered — sending it back

Start soft — I'm afraid …, Sorry, but …, I think … — then say what's wrong, then what you'd like. a bit makes a problem sound smaller: it's a bit cold. For a wait that's still going on, lesson 7: We've been waiting for …

What's wrong The words Say it out loud
the wrong dish I'm afraid this isn't what I ordered. I'm afraid this isn't what I ordered — I ordered the fish, not the chicken.
something about it Sorry, but it's a bit … Sorry, but my soup is a bit cold. Could you heat it up?
a long wait We've been waiting for … Excuse me — we've been waiting for forty minutes. Is our food coming?
the check I think there's a mistake on the check. I think there's a mistake on the check — we didn't have a dessert.
in a store I'd like to return this. I'd like to return this. I bought it on Saturday, and it doesn't work.
what you want a refund · exchange it Could I get a refund? Or could I exchange it for a different one?
I asked a steak.
I ordered a steak.
In a restaurant, food is ordered. If you use ask, it needs for: I asked for a steak.
I want my money back.
Could I get a refund, please?
The first one starts a fight. Your money back is a refund.
I'd like to devolve this.
I'd like to return this.
Taking something back to the store is return: I returned the shoes on Monday.
4 · Can I try it on? — shopping and paying

fit is the size: it fits, it doesn't fit. suit is how it looks on you: it suits you, it looks good on you. try on works like the phone verbs from lesson 6 — a pronoun goes in the middle: try it on.

What you're saying The words Say it out loud
not now I'm just looking. Can I help you? — I'm just looking, thanks. Maybe later.
another one Do you have this in …? Do you have this in a medium? Do you have it in blue?
trying Can I try it on? Can I try these on? Where can I try them on?
the size It fits · It doesn't fit It doesn't fit — it's too tight. This one fits perfectly.
how it looks It suits you · It looks good on you That color really suits you. It looks good on you.
paying by card · in cash · in installments Can I pay by card? Can I pay in three installments?
Can I prove it?
Can I try it on?
Clothes are tried on. Food is tried or tasted. prove is to show that something is true.
Can I pay with card?
Can I pay by card?
by card, in cash — no a, no with.
It stays good on you.
It looks good on you.
How clothes look on someone: it looks good on you, or it suits you.
Language Tip
I'd like vs. I like
Both have like — the 'd changes everything. I like is how you feel in general: always, every time. I'd like is what you want right now — a polite I want, the one to use when you order, ask or buy. So ask yourself: am I talking about my taste, or asking for something now?
I like — in general, always
I like + a thing · …ing
  • I like Italian food.
  • I like this restaurant — I come here every week.
  • Do you like spicy food? — Yes, I love it.
I'd like — now, please
I'd like + a thing · to + verb
  • I'd like a table for two, please.
  • I'd like to try it on.
  • Would you like a dessert? — Yes, please.
The trap

✗ I like a coffee, please.  →  ✓ I'd like a coffee, please.

I like a coffee says coffee is something you enjoy — it doesn't ask for one. The 'd is small, but it's the whole request, so say it clearly. Questions work the same way: Do you like coffee? asks about your taste, but Would you like a coffee? is an offer — answer it with Yes, please or No, thanks, never Yes, I like.

Both sides — say all four out loud
  1. I like this place — I'd like the same table as last time.
  2. Would you like a dessert? — No, thanks. I'd just like the check.
  3. Do you like spicy food? — Yes, I love it. I'd like the curry, please.
  4. I like this jacket — I'd like to try it on in a medium.
