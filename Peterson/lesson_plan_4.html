What's it like where you live?

The conversation this whole lesson is for, and you have it for real — nothing here is for repeating. I ask you about where you live, from the first row. You answer by finishing one of the second row with something true, and keep it going with the third. Then we swap: you ask me. My first answers will be short — It's OK. — so get three real details out of me before you close it.

1 · I ask you
What's your neighborhood like? Is there a … near your house? Are there any good places to eat? How far is it from your work? What's your place like?
2 · You answer — finish one with something true
It's quite …, and there's a … around the corner. There are a lot of …, but there aren't many … There isn't much …, so … It's about … minutes from … by … The best thing about it is that there's … It isn't as … as …, but …
3 · You keep it going
What about you? Where do you live? Is there a … near you? Is it far from your work? What's it like at night? Do you like it there?
4 · Swap — you ask, then close it
Sounds nice. That's really convenient. That sounds a bit far! I'd love to live somewhere like that.
Stage 02 of 04
Your places toolkit
Four blocks, easiest first. Each one is a way to say what's in a place, and where.
1 · there is, there are

To say that something is in a place. One thing: there's. More than one: there are. To ask, swap the two words round: Is there …? Are there any …? To say no: there isn't a …, there aren't any …. The boxed words take the same shape into the past and the future.

Pattern Example Say it out loud
there's a … There's a bakery on my street. There's a bakery on my street, and there's a pharmacy next to it.
there are … There are two bathrooms. There are two bathrooms in my apartment, and there are three bedrooms.
there isn't a … There isn't a gym in my building. There isn't a gym in my building, and there isn't a pool either.
there aren't any … There aren't any shops near here. There aren't any shops near here, and there aren't any buses after ten.
Is there a …? Is there a pharmacy near here? Is there a pharmacy near here? — Yes, there is. There's one around the corner.
Are there any …? Are there any good restaurants in the area? Are there any good restaurants in the area? — No, there aren't, but there are a few nice cafés.
The same shape, at other times
there was a … there were some … there's going to be a … there'll be … there might be …
Have a gym in my building.
There's a gym in my building.
To say something is in a place, start with there's or there are. has needs an owner in front of it: My building has a gym.
Is there any good restaurants near here?
Are there any good restaurants near here?
The word after it decides: one thing, is there; more than one, are there.
2 · a lot of, not much, a few

How much of something there is. Some things you can count — buses, shops, people — and some you can't — traffic, noise, parking. a lot of works for both. Things you can count take many and a few; things you can't count take much. In a negative or a question, some becomes any.

How much The words Say it out loud
lots — of anything a lot of There are a lot of buses, and there's a lot of traffic.
a small number a few There are a few good cafés near the office, and a few nice restaurants.
not a lot — you can count it not many There aren't many buses after ten, and there aren't many taxis either.
not a lot — you can't count it not much There isn't much parking near the office, and there isn't much space in the kitchen.
zero no · not any There's no parking on my street, and there aren't any shops.
more than you want too much · too many There's too much noise at night, and there are too many cars.
You can count them
buses cars people shops trees
You can't count it
traffic noise parking space pollution
There are many traffic in the morning.
There's a lot of traffic in the morning.
You can't count traffic, so it takes there's. In a positive sentence, a lot of sounds the most natural.
There isn't nothing near my house.
There isn't anything near my house.
One negative per sentence: There isn't anything, or There's nothing — never both.
There are a lot of peoples on the metro.
There are a lot of people on the metro.
people is already more than one: one person, two people — never peoples.
3 · Where it is

Saying exactly where something is. Most of these are two or three words that travel together — learn them whole: next to, around the corner. A building has floors, and floors take on. For how far, give the time and the way: ten minutes on foot.

Where Example Say it out loud
next to The kitchen is next to the meeting room. The kitchen is next to the meeting room, and the restrooms are next to the kitchen.
opposite The bank is opposite the bakery. The bank is opposite the bakery, and the metro is opposite the bank.
between The pharmacy is between the bank and the bakery. The pharmacy is between the bank and the bakery, on the same side as the bus stop.
around the corner There's a gym around the corner. There's a gym around the corner, and a supermarket just around the next corner.
on the … floor My office is on the fifth floor. My office is on the fifth floor, and the meeting rooms are on the sixth.
… minutes from It's ten minutes from here on foot. It's ten minutes from here on foot, or two minutes by car.
How far is it?
within walking distance five minutes' walk ten minutes by car two stops on the metro close to a long way from
My office is in the fifth floor.
My office is on the fifth floor.
Floors take on: on the first floor, on the top floor. Streets too: on my street.
It's near of my house.
It's near my house.
near goes straight to the place. Its partners keep their small word: close to, next to.
4 · Words for a home, an office and a neighborhood

Words for the three places you spend your week. Learn each one with the small word that goes with it: in the living room, on the balcony, at my desk.

At home
a two-bedroom apartment the living room a balcony the doorman a parking space a view of the park
At work
the reception my desk the meeting room the kitchen the restrooms
In the neighborhood
a bakery a pharmacy a bus stop a square a shopping mall a metro station
I stayed in home on Sunday.
I stayed at home on Sunday.
at home — no in, no the. The same with at work and at school.
We went in the cinema last night.
We went to the cinema last night.
Going somewhere takes to: to the gym, to work, to the bank. One exception: go home, with nothing in between.
Language Tip
there's vs. it's
Every place you describe goes in the same order: first you say that something is there, then you say what it's like. Those are two different jobs, and English gives each one its own opening. there's puts something new in the picture. it's talks about something that is already in it. And an English sentence never starts with the verb — one of these two words always comes first.
something new — it's there
there's a … · there are …
  • There's a park near my house.
  • There are two bakeries on my street.
  • There's a lot of traffic in the morning.
something you already said — what it's like
it's … · they're …
  • It's small, but it's very quiet.
  • They're both open until ten.
  • It's terrible after six.
The trap

✗ Have a park near my house. Is very big.  →  ✓ There's a park near my house. It's very big.

Every sentence needs a word before the verb — and that word says which job the sentence is doing. there's says it exists; it's describes it. has is fine too, but only with an owner in front of it: My building has a gym. In a short answer, keep the same opening word: Is there a gym? — Yes, there is. Is it big? — Yes, it is.

Both sides — say all four out loud
  1. There's a bakery on the corner. It's small, but the bread is fantastic.
  2. There are two parks near my house. They're both quiet in the morning.
  3. There's a meeting room on the fifth floor. It's opposite the elevators.
  4. Is there a gym in your building? Is it free?
