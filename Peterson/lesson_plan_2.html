Friday afternoon, every week

The conversation this whole lesson is for, and you have it for real, about your actual weekend — nothing here is for repeating. I ask you one question from the first row. You answer by finishing one of the second row with something true, and keep it going with the third. Then we swap: you ask me. My first answer will be short on purpose — get two more details out of me before you close it.

1 · I ask you
Any plans for the weekend? Are you doing anything nice? What are you up to on Saturday? Are you free on Sunday?
2 · You answer — finish one with something true
I'm going to … I'm seeing … on Saturday. Not much. I'll probably just … I might … if the weather's good. I'd like to …, but … I'm working on …, unfortunately.
3 · You keep it going
What about you? Sounds great. Lucky you! Where are you going? Who are you going with?
4 · Swap — you ask, then close it
Have a good weekend! Have fun! Enjoy it! See you on Monday.

Your future toolkit
Seven blocks, easiest first. Each one is a piece of next week.
1 · going to

For plans you have already made — the decision is behind you, only the doing is ahead. Three parts, and none of them is optional: am / is / are, then going to, then the base verb. I read the first row of the table, then the italic sentences are yours; the boxed words are mine first and yours after, twice each.

Usually Tomorrow Say it out loud
I work from home. I'm going to work from home. I'm going to work from home tomorrow. I'm going to start early, and I'm going to finish early.
She takes the bus. She's going to take the bus. She's going to take the bus tomorrow. She's going to take it at seven, and she's going to get there at eight.
We have a meeting. We're going to have a meeting. We're going to have a meeting tomorrow. We're going to talk about the budget, and we're going to make a decision.
They don't open on Sundays. They aren't going to open. They aren't going to open tomorrow. They aren't going to answer the phone, and they aren't going to reply to emails.
Do you stay late? Are you going to stay late? Are you going to stay late tomorrow? Are you going to finish the report, or are you going to leave it for Friday?
It rains a lot. It's going to rain. It's going to rain tomorrow. It's going to rain all day, so I'm going to take an umbrella.
The whole verb
I'm going to you're going to he's / she's going to we're going to they're going to
Saying no
I'm not going to she isn't going to we aren't going to they aren't going to
Asking
Are you going to …? Is she going to …? What are you going to do? When are you going to …? Where are you going to …?
What you will actually hear — listen only
I'm gonna call her. What are you gonna do? It's gonna rain.

That last group is listen only: I say them, you don't need to say them back. gonna is how going to sounds at speed — understand it everywhere, and say going to for now.

I go to call you tomorrow.
I'm going to call you tomorrow.
Not go to: going to, with am in front of it.
Next week I travel to Recife.
Next week I'm going to travel to Recife.
The present alone sounds like a timetable. Your own plans need going to.
She's going to works late.
She's going to work late.
After to, always the base verb — no -s, no -ed, no -ing.
What you are going to do?
What are you going to do?
Question word, then are, then the person — the same order as What did you do?
2 · want to, would like to, hoping to, thinking of

Not every plan is decided. These say how far along it is, from an idea to a decision. Nearly all of them take to and the base verb; the one to watch is thinking of, which takes -ing. Italic sentences are yours after my first row; boxed words mine first, then yours, twice each.

How far along The words Say it out loud
just an idea I'm thinking of + -ing I'm thinking of changing jobs. I'm thinking of looking in São Paulo, and I'm thinking of starting next year.
a wish I'd like to · I'd love to I'd like to learn Spanish. I'd love to live abroad one day, and I'd like to start soon.
a hope I'm hoping to I'm hoping to leave early on Friday. I'm hoping to leave at four, and I'm hoping to miss the traffic.
a plan I'm planning to I'm planning to take a week off in December. I'm planning to go to the beach, and I'm planning to switch off my phone.
decided I'm going to I'm going to talk to my manager tomorrow. I'm going to talk to her in the morning, before the meeting.
The polite way to want
I'd like to … Would you like to …? What would you like to do? I'd love to. I'd rather not.
Someone else's plan for you
My manager wants me to … She'd like me to … They want us to … Do you want me to …?
I want that you come to the meeting.
I want you to come to the meeting.
want + a person + to. There is no that after want.
I like to go to Italy next year.
I'd like to go to Italy next year.
I like is always true. I'd like is something you want to happen.
I'm thinking to change jobs.
I'm thinking of changing jobs.
of, then -ing. It is the one on this list that works that way.
Would you like go for a coffee?
Would you like to go for a coffee?
The to stays, even in a question.

4 · will: offers and promises

will does most of its work in offers and promises — the things you say when someone needs something. It is almost always 'll, and the negative is won't. Italic sentences are yours after my first row; boxed lines mine first, then yours, twice each.

What just happened What you say Say it out loud
The phone is ringing. I'll get it. I'll get it. Don't worry, I'll get it — I'll tell them you're in a meeting.
"I don't have the file." I'll send it to you. I'll send it to you now. I'll send it by email, and I'll call you if there's a problem.
"What would you like?" I'll have the chicken. I'll have the chicken, please. And I'll have a water — no, I'll have a juice.
You're running late. I'll be there in five minutes. I'll be there in five minutes. I'll be quick, and I won't be late again.
"Please don't tell anyone." I won't tell anyone. I won't tell anyone. I won't say a word, and I won't forget.
Offering
I'll do it. I'll call you back. Shall I send it to you? Shall I open the window? Do you want me to help?
Suggesting
Shall we start? Shall we get a coffee? Let's do it on Monday. Why don't we meet on Friday?
Promising
I'll be there. I won't be late. I'll let you know. I'll do my best.
I help you with that.
I'll help you with that.
An offer needs 'll. Without it, it sounds like something you do every day.
Do I open the window?
Shall I open the window?
Offering to do something: Shall I …? Asking about both of you: Shall we …?
I will to call you tomorrow.
I'll call you tomorrow.
No to after will. And say 'll — the full will sounds like you are insisting.
won't · said like want
won't · rhymes with don't
want is a wish. won't is will not. Say it after me, twice each.
5 · How sure are you?

A plan is rarely a hundred per cent. These sit between yes and no, and they make an answer sound honest instead of blunt. Watch where probably goes — it moves. Italic sentences are yours after my first row; boxed words mine first, then yours, twice each.

How sure The words Say it out loud
certain I'll definitely … I'll definitely be there. I'll definitely be on time, and I'll definitely bring the numbers.
very likely I'll probably … I'll probably work from home tomorrow. I'll probably start early, and I'll probably finish at five.
maybe I might … I might go to the gym later. I might go after work, or I might go tomorrow morning.
not likely I probably won't … I probably won't finish today. I probably won't have time, so I'll finish it tomorrow.
what should happen It should … It should be ready by Friday. It should be easy, and it shouldn't take long.
the plan — but … It's supposed to … The meeting is supposed to finish at five. It's supposed to be short, but it never is.
When you haven't decided
I'm not sure yet. It depends. We'll see. Probably not. I hope so. I'll let you know.
I will probably not go.
I probably won't go.
probably goes after 'll but before won't: I'll probably go, I probably won't go.
Maybe I'll go, maybe no.
Maybe I'll go, maybe not.
no is an answer on its own. Inside a sentence it is not: maybe not, I hope not.
I might to come later.
I might come later.
Like will: no to after might.
I suppose to finish it today.
I'm supposed to finish it today.
am / is / are, then supposed — with a d you can barely hear.
6 · If, when, as soon as

Two halves: what has to happen first, and what happens after it. The half that starts with if, when, as soon as, before, after, until or unless stays in the present, even though it is about the future. The will lives in the other half. Italic sentences are yours after my first row.

Starts with The whole sentence Say it out loud
if If it rains, I'll stay at home. If it rains, I'll stay at home. If it doesn't rain, I'll go to the beach.
when When I get home, I'll call you. When I get home, I'll call you. When I call you, I'll tell you everything.
as soon as I'll send it as soon as I finish. I'll send it as soon as I finish. As soon as I send it, I'll let you know.
before · after I'll have a coffee before the meeting starts. I'll have a coffee before the meeting starts. After it finishes, I'll have another one.
until I'll wait until you arrive. I'll wait here until you arrive. I won't start until everyone is here.
unless — if not I'll come unless I have to work late. I'll come unless I have to work late. Unless something changes, I'll see you at eight.

Last week's joining words work for the future too. Boxed: mine first, then yours, twice each.

Telling a plan in order
First, I'm going to … Then I'll … After that, As soon as that's done, Finally, And that's the plan.
If I will have time, I'll call you.
If I have time, I'll call you.
One will per sentence, and it never goes straight after if.
I'll call you when I will arrive.
I'll call you when I arrive.
The same rule with when, as soon as, before, after and until.
I'll wait you here.
I'll wait for you here.
wait always takes for before a person or a thing.
I'll go unless it doesn't rain.
I'll go unless it rains.
unless already means if not. Don't add a second not.

Language Tip
will, might vs. going to, want to
Every future on this page has two parts: a word that points forward, then the verb. The part that goes wrong is the join. Some of these words take to before the verb and some never do — and it is fixed for each word, never a choice. Get the join right and every sentence on this page comes out right.
no to — straight to the verb
will · might · should + the base verb
  • I'll call you tomorrow.
  • I might come later.
  • It should be ready by Friday.
always to
going to · want to · would like to + the base verb
  • I'm going to work from home.
  • I want to change jobs.
  • Would you like to come?
The trap

✗ I will to call you.  →  ✓ I'll call you.  ·  ✗ Would you like go for a coffee?  →  ✓ Would you like to go for a coffee?

will, might and should never take to; going to, want to and would like to always keep it — even in a question. After all six, the verb is the base form: no -s, no -ed, no -ing. The two on this page that break the pattern, thinking of and looking forward to, take -ing — learn those two whole.

  2. I might work from home on Friday.
  3. I'm going to take a few days off in November.
  4. Would you like to come for dinner on Saturday?

One short scene

You play yourself. I play a colleague who needs an hour of your week, and your week is already full. Your diary and your half are below — you get a minute to read them. My half stays hidden until we start, because in real life it would be.

You — with a full diary
  • Say what is in your diary with the -ing form: I'm flying, I'm meeting.
  • Say no politely, then offer something else — one I'll … and one Shall we …?
  • Say how sure you are about Wednesday: probably, might.
  • Use if or as soon as at least once.
  • Agree a day and a time, and say it back to check.
Me — a colleague who needs an hour
Hidden until the scene starts.
  • In a hurry, and wants to meet in person, not online.
  • "Are you free this afternoon?"
  • "Can't you leave the course early?"
  • Pushes for Tuesday at four.
  • Won't accept a maybe: "So is that a yes or a no?"
  • Halfway through: the client has moved the presentation to Thursday afternoon — when you are supposed to be in Brasília.
