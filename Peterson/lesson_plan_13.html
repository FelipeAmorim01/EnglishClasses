Stage 02 of 04
Your advice toolkit
Three blocks: giving advice, rules and duties, and looking back.
1 · If I were you, I'd … — giving advice

should is the normal way to give advice, with no to after it. If I were you, I'd … and Why don't you …? are softer — you're only offering an idea. you'd better is different: it's a warning. More on that at the end of this stage.

What you're saying The words Say it out loud
advice should · shouldn't You should talk to her. You shouldn't work so late. You should take a break.
asking for advice Should I …? · What should I do? Should I take the job? Should I ask first? What should I do?
softer — if it was you If I were you, I'd … If I were you, I'd wait. If I were you, I'd say no.
an idea Why don't you …? · Have you tried …ing? Why don't you call him? Have you tried asking your boss?
recommending I'd recommend …ing · I suggest you … I'd recommend booking early. I suggest you ask first.
a warning you'd better … · you'd better not … You'd better leave now — it's late. You'd better not tell him.
Can you give me an advice?
Can you give me some advice?
advice has no a and no -s: some advice, a piece of advice, good advice.
I suggest you to go by train.
I suggest you go by train.
No to after suggest you — or say I suggest going by train.
You should to call him.
You should call him.
No to after should — the same as can and will.
2 · I have to, I'm supposed to — rules and duties

have to is for rules and duties — at work, at home. Its past is had to. must is stronger and more formal: you see it on signs and in written rules. I'm supposed to means it's the rule — but it doesn't always happen. And careful: don't have to means there's no need. It does not mean not allowed.

What you're saying The words Say it out loud
a rule or a duty I have to … · she has to … I have to start at eight. She has to be there at nine.
no need I don't have to … · I don't need to … I don't have to wear a suit. I don't need to go in on Fridays.
asking about rules Do you have to …? · Does she have to …? Do you have to work on Saturdays? Does she have to travel?
yesterday · tomorrow had to · will have to I had to stay late yesterday, so I'll have to leave early tomorrow.
the rule — but … I'm supposed to …, but … I'm supposed to start at eight, but I usually get in at a quarter past.
written rules must · must not Visitors must sign in. You must not park here.
I musted work late yesterday.
I had to work late yesterday.
must has no past. Use had to.
Do you must wear a uniform?
Do you have to wear a uniform?
Questions about rules use have to: Do you have to …?
It's optional — you mustn't come.
It's optional — you don't have to come.
don't have to = no need. mustn't = not allowed.
3 · I should have called — looking back

To look back at a mistake, use should have + the third form — the same form as in I've gone and I'd gone. Listen and repeat only: in speech, should have sounds like should've, and could have like could've.

What you're saying The words Say it out loud
you didn't do it — a mistake I should have … I should have left earlier. I should have called her.
you did it — a mistake I shouldn't have … I shouldn't have eaten so much. I shouldn't have said that.
telling someone You should have … You should have told me! You should have asked.
it was possible I could have … I could have taken a taxi. I could have asked for help.
I should have went to the doctor.
I should have gone to the doctor.
The third form: gone, like I've gone.
Yesterday I should call him.
Yesterday I should have called him.
Looking back always needs have.
Language Tip
You should vs. You'd better
Both tell someone what to do, but they don't sound the same. should is just a good idea — friendly, and fine with anyone. you'd better is a warning: do it, or there's a problem. It looks past, but it's about now or soon.
A good idea — should
You should + verb.
  • You should try the fish here.
  • You should visit Lisbon one day.
  • You should get some rest this weekend.
Or there's a problem — you'd better
You'd better + verb.
  • You'd better hurry — the train leaves at six.
  • You'd better take an umbrella — it's going to rain.
  • You'd better not be late again.
The trap

✗ To a dinner guest: "You'd better try the fish."  →  ✓ "You should try the fish."

With you'd better, something bad happens if they don't — so to a guest, it sounds almost like a threat. Keep it for real problems: time, money, trouble. And no to after it: you'd better go, never you'd better to go.

Both sides — say all four out loud
  1. You should come to the party — it'll be fun.
  2. You'd better leave now, or you'll miss the bus.
  3. You should read that book. You'll love it.
  4. We'd better not park here — they'll give us a ticket.
