Stage 01 of 05
Warm-up: Where to next?
Answer in full stories, not single sentences.
Let's talk
  • Tell me about the last trip you took — work or holiday. Where did you go, and what stayed with you?
  • What kind of traveller are you: plan every detail, or figure it out when you land?
  • What's the worst thing that has ever gone wrong on a trip? How did you handle it?
  • Is it easier for you to talk to strangers abroad or here at home? Why do you think that is?
  • If the right job appeared tomorrow, which country would you move to — and which one would you never move to?
Stage 02 of 05
Travel Toolkit
Textbook English gets you understood. This is the English that makes you sound like you've done this before.
Say it like a traveller
Textbook version What people actually say In context
"I want a table for two." Could we get a table for two? · Party of two, please. "Hi — could we get a table for two, somewhere quiet if possible?" In the US the host usually asks "How many in your party?" — the short, natural answer is "Party of two, please."
"Where is the bus stop?" Do you know where I'd catch the bus to...? "Sorry to bother you — do you know where I'd catch the bus to the centre?"
"I don't understand." Sorry, could you run that by me again? "Sorry, could you run that by me again? It's a bit loud in here."
"The room is bad." The room isn't quite what we booked. "I'm afraid the room isn't quite what we booked — we asked for a double."
"My flight is late." My flight's been delayed / They've pushed it back. "My flight's been delayed — they've pushed it back a couple of hours."
"I liked the trip." It was one of those trips you don't want to end. "Honestly, it was one of those trips you don't want to end."
Phrasal verbs that live in airports
The long way The natural way Example
to depart / begin a journey set off "We set off at five to beat the traffic."
to arrive (a flight, a train) get in "My flight gets in around eleven at night."
to register at a hotel / leave it check in · check out "I'll check in, drop my bags off, and meet you there."
to break a journey in another city stop over "We stopped over in Lisbon for a night."
to go away for a short break get away "We're trying to get away for a few days in October."
to explore a place casually look around "We had a few hours to look around the old town."
to collect someone pick (someone) up "Can you pick me up at arrivals? I'll text you when I land."
Idiom spotlight — travel talk
off the beaten track
Away from the tourist route; somewhere few people go.
"We stayed in a village off the beaten track — no tour buses at all."
hit the road
To leave, to start the journey.
"We should hit the road before it gets dark."
travel light
To take very little luggage with you.
"I've learned to travel light — hand luggage only."
get itchy feet
To feel restless, like you need to travel again.
"After six months in the same city I start getting itchy feet."
a whistle-stop tour
A very fast visit to many places, with little time in each.
"It was a whistle-stop tour — four cities in five days."
live out of a suitcase
To travel so constantly that you never really unpack.
"That job had me living out of a suitcase for two years."
Stage 03 of 05
The Small Talk Engine
Small talk isn't about being interesting. It's about giving the other person something easy to hold on to.
The four-step ladder
Every good small-talk exchange follows the same shape. Learn the shape once and you'll never be stuck at a conference dinner again.
  1. Observe. Comment on something you're both experiencing right now. — "This queue is unbelievable, isn't it?"
  2. Ask. Turn it into an open question. — "Have you flown this route before?"
  3. Give. When they ask you back, never answer in one word. Give them a detail to grab. — "I have, actually — I come out here twice a year for work."
  4. Bridge. Use their detail to open a new topic. — "You mentioned you live here — where would you eat tonight if you were me?"
The one-word trap
Dead end Keeps it alive Why it works
"Yes." / "It was good." "It was great, actually — especially the..." Gives the other person a hook to ask about.
"Do you like it here?" "What's it like living here?" Open question — impossible to answer with yes or no.
"I'm an engineer." "I'm an engineer — I work on X, which basically means..." Turns a job title into a conversation.
"Oh, nice." "Oh really? How come?" Shows interest and hands the turn back to them.
Safe ground vs. thin ice
Safe with almost anyone
  • The situation you're both in (the flight, the queue, the event)
  • Travel — where they've been, where they'd go again
  • Food and restaurants, local recommendations
  • Work, in general terms — what they do, how they got into it
  • Films, series, music, sport
  • Weekend and holiday plans
  • The city you're in — what's changed, what's worth seeing
Handle with care
  • Politics and religion
  • Salary, the price of their house, money in general
  • Age, weight, appearance
  • Marital status, children ("So when are you having kids?")
  • Health problems
  • Strong criticism of their country or their city
  • Anything you'd only ask a close friend
Rule of thumb: if the honest answer could embarrass them, it's not small talk.
Reactions that buy you time
Oh really? No way! That sounds amazing. Same here. Tell me about it. How come? What's that like? I know what you mean. That must have been tough. Good for you!
Getting out politely
It was really nice talking to you. I'd better let you get back to it. I'm going to grab another coffee — enjoy the rest of the day. Let's stay in touch — are you on LinkedIn? I should probably go and say hello to a few people.
Stage 04 of 05
Out in the World
Three roleplays. You play one side — I'll play the other. Two are problems to solve; the last one is pure conversation, which is usually the harder of the two.
How it works

You only see your own brief. Stay in role until the situation is resolved — and keep the language from stages 2 and 3 within reach.

Scenario A — The Room You Didn't Book Guest vs. Front Desk
It's 11 p.m. You've just come off a twelve-hour flight and you're standing at the reception desk of your hotel.
Your role — Guest
  • You booked and paid for a quiet double room with air conditioning.
  • You've been given a small twin room facing a main road, and the AC doesn't work.
  • You have an important meeting at 9 a.m. and you need to sleep.
  • You want: the room you booked. If that's impossible, you want real compensation.
  • Challenge: stay polite the whole way through. Firm, but never rude.
Counterpart — Front Desk
Hidden.
  • The hotel is genuinely full — a conference took the last rooms.
  • The booking system double-sold the double rooms. It isn't the guest's fault and you know it.
  • You CAN offer: a fan tonight, a free upgrade from tomorrow, breakfast included, late checkout, dinner on the house, or a taxi to a partner hotel ten minutes away.
  • You cannot refund tonight without the manager, who is back at 8 a.m.
  • If the guest stays calm and specific, be generous. If they shout, retreat to policy.
Scenario B — The Missed Connection Passenger vs. Airline Agent
Your first flight landed two hours late. Your connection left without you. You're at the transfer desk with a queue behind you.
Your role — Passenger
  • You must be at a client meeting tomorrow at 10 a.m. — missing it is not an option.
  • Your checked bag is somewhere in the system.
  • You want: the fastest route there, plus a hotel and food if you have to wait.
  • Ask about your rights — the delay was the airline's fault.
  • Challenge: get what you need without ever raising your voice.
Counterpart — Airline Agent
Hidden.
  • The next direct flight is tomorrow at 2 p.m. — too late for the meeting.
  • There's one seat tonight via another city, arriving 6 a.m. It means no sleep, but it works.
  • You can issue a hotel voucher and meal vouchers, but only if the passenger stays overnight.
  • The bag can be rerouted, but it may arrive a day later.
  • You have twenty people waiting. Be helpful, but keep it moving — mention the queue if it drags.
Scenario C — The Conference Dinner You vs. a Stranger
You've been seated next to someone you've never met. There are forty minutes until the speeches. No problem to solve — just the conversation.
Your role — You
  • Open the conversation. Don't wait to be spoken to.
  • Climb the ladder: observe → ask → give → bridge.
  • Mission: find out three real things about them, and find one thing you have in common.
  • Then exit politely — without it feeling like an escape.
  • Forbidden: one-word answers, and asking "So, what do you do?" in the first thirty seconds.
Counterpart — The Stranger
Hidden.
  • You're shy. Closed questions get short, flat answers.
  • Open questions get real answers — and you warm up quickly once someone tries.
  • Facts to reveal only if asked well: you're from Vancouver, you work in logistics, you moved to São Paulo four months ago, you cycle competitively, and you are quietly hating this dinner.
  • If they ask two good follow-up questions in a row, open up and start asking questions back.
Useful language
Complaining politely
I'm afraid there's been a mix-up. This isn't quite what we agreed when I booked. I understand it's not your fault, but... Is there anything you can do for me?
Being firm
I do need this sorted out tonight. Could I speak to someone who can? What are my options here, realistically?
Small talk openers
Is this seat taken? Have you come far for this? First time at one of these? How do you know the organisers?
Keeping it going
What's that like? How did you get into that? Whereabouts is that? You mentioned... tell me more.
